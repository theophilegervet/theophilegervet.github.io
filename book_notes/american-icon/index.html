American Icon — Alan Mulally and the Fight to Save Ford Motor Company

1. Mulally’s Operating System: One Team, One Plan, One Goal

Mulally’s central contribution was a repeatable management system for running a complex global company. Developed at Boeing and adapted at Ford, it combined a single business plan, standardized performance reporting, weekly reviews, explicit leadership behaviors, and cross-functional problem-solving.

Ford already had talented executives and engineers. But its divisions operated independently, executives concealed problems, and different groups used different numbers. Mulally’s system forced the organization to work from the same facts toward the same objectives.

The Business Plan Review (BPR)

Mulally replaced Ford’s sprawling monthly executive meeting cycle with one mandatory corporate-level review every Thursday at 8 AM.

  • One common business plan: Every function and region reported against the same company-wide goals rather than optimizing its own agenda.
  • Standardized slides: Reports showed targets, actual performance, forecasts, and changes from the previous week. Different executives could no longer present incompatible versions of reality.
  • Traffic-light status: Green = on plan; yellow = concern; red = off plan. Split-color boxes displayed changes in status between weeks.
  • Executive ownership: Each leader personally presented their business. Subordinates could attend but could not answer questions on their boss’s behalf.
  • Succinct updates, not lengthy debates: The BPR established the overall business picture. Problems requiring detailed discussion moved to Special Attention Reviews (SARs) immediately afterward.
  • Fixed weekly cadence: Executives returned every Thursday with updated information. Unresolved issues and commitments remained visible.
  • No politics: No side conversations, jokes at colleagues’ expense, personal attacks, or BlackBerry distractions.

Mulally gave executives Boeing slide templates to establish a consistent reporting process. He emphasized facts, forecasts, and changes rather than elaborate explanations.

The distinction: The BPR was not primarily a forum for making every decision. It was the mechanism for ensuring the entire leadership team understood the business, recognized problems, and coordinated action.

Special Attention Reviews (SARs)

The SAR separated detailed problem-solving from the main business review.

  1. The BPR exposed an issue requiring attention.
  2. Relevant leaders examined the underlying facts and alternatives.
  3. The discussion focused on solving the business problem rather than assigning political blame.
  4. Subsequent reviews tracked whether the issue was being resolved.

This prevented one issue from consuming the time needed to understand the entire business.

The Ten Rules of Working Together

Mulally introduced ten principles at the first BPR, importing them from Boeing:

  1. People first
  2. Everyone is included
  3. Compelling vision
  4. Clear performance goals
  5. One plan
  6. Facts and data
  7. Propose a plan, “find-a-way” attitude
  8. Respect, listen, help, and appreciate each other
  9. Emotional resilience — trust the process
  10. Have fun — enjoy the journey and each other

These were operating expectations, not simply values for a corporate presentation. Mulally reinforced them through meeting rules and his responses to individual executives.

The Red-Slide Moment

Initially, every executive reported green despite Ford being headed toward billions in losses.

Mulally challenged the contradiction: how could the company be performing so well against its plans while losing so much money?

Mark Fields eventually reported the Ford Edge launch as red after engineers identified a suspension problem that threatened shipments.

At the old Ford, revealing such a problem publicly could damage an executive’s career. Instead, Mulally praised Fields for disclosing it and asked the other executives how they could help.

The issue was resolved, and Edge shipments began in December 2006.

What changed: Executives learned that identifying problems would bring assistance rather than automatic punishment. Red indicators began appearing in subsequent reviews.

The standard for reporting became more honest without reducing the expectation that problems would be solved.

The One Ford Wallet Card

Mulally worked with HR executive Felicia Fields to distill the company’s direction into a small blue card distributed to employees.

One Team: People working together as a lean, global enterprise for automotive leadership, measured through the satisfaction of customers, employees, dealers, investors, suppliers, unions, and communities.

One Plan: Four priorities:

  1. Aggressively restructure to operate profitably at current demand and changing model mix.
  2. Accelerate development of products customers want and value.
  3. Finance the plan and improve the balance sheet.
  4. Work together effectively as one team.

One Goal: An exciting, viable Ford delivering profitable growth for all.

The reverse listed four expected behaviors:

  • Foster Functional and Technical Excellence
  • Own Working Together
  • Role Model Ford Values
  • Deliver Results

Mulally repeated the plan in executive meetings, employee communications, investor presentations, and interviews. He resisted constantly introducing new strategies while the existing one was still being implemented.

What Made His System Different

  • One operating picture: All senior leaders worked from common facts, targets, and forecasts.
  • Transparency without humiliation: Bad news was expected to be surfaced, not hidden.
  • Collective problem-solving: Leaders were expected to help other functions succeed.
  • Personal accountability: Executives owned their numbers and commitments.
  • Separation of reporting and decisions: BPR established visibility; SAR handled detailed issues.
  • Relentless consistency: The same plan and process continued through restructuring, financial crisis, and recovery.

2. Changing Ford’s Culture: From Internal Politics to Working Together

Ford’s dysfunction had deep historical roots. Henry Ford drove away talented executives who challenged him. Henry Ford II modernized the company but eventually encouraged rivalry among senior managers. Over time, political survival became an important executive skill.

By 2006, regional divisions guarded their resources, executives undermined one another, and admitting problems was dangerous.

Mulally’s Approach

  • Eliminate political behavior: Executives were not permitted to ridicule colleagues or use meetings to undermine rivals.
  • Make facts more important than hierarchy: Business performance had to be explained with data rather than authority or political influence.
  • Enforce participation: Senior executives could not exempt themselves from the BPR because of their status or other commitments.
  • Retain capable insiders: Mulally initially worked with Ford’s existing leadership rather than immediately replacing everyone with outsiders.
  • Model accessibility: He ate with employees, visited workplaces, invited questions, and communicated directly beyond the executive suite.
  • Examine products personally: He drove Ford vehicles and then competitors’ cars. He discovered that Ford executives rarely drove competitors’ vehicles and encouraged them to start doing so.

Mulally’s insistence on cooperation did not mean avoiding difficult decisions. Leaders remained responsible for performance and were expected to adapt to the new system.

The cultural change depended on repeated behavior: Employees had to see that the new rules were enforced and that transparency was genuinely safer than concealment.

3. One Ford: Redesigning the Organization

Ford had become a collection of regional businesses that duplicated engineering, purchasing, manufacturing, and product development.

North America sometimes continued selling outdated vehicles while Europe had newer, more competitive products. Regional executives often resisted sharing platforms and technology because doing so threatened their control.

Global Integration

Mulally reorganized Ford around global functions and regional business operations.

  • Global product development: Derrick Kuzak coordinated engineering and vehicle programs across regions.
  • Global purchasing: Consolidated purchasing increased scale and reduced duplicated supplier relationships.
  • Global manufacturing: Shared processes and capabilities improved consistency and efficiency.
  • Regional operations: Local teams remained responsible for understanding customers and executing in their markets.
  • Common management process: Functions and regions reported through the One Ford operating system.

Mulally wanted a full family of small, medium, and large cars, utilities, and trucks rather than a business dependent primarily on large American vehicles.

He also wanted a more balanced global business across the Americas, Europe, and Asia.

Reducing Product Complexity

Hoffman describes Mulally discovering that Ford had numerous different hood structures and latches where common designs could reduce unnecessary variation.

Similar duplication existed throughout vehicle development.

Global platforms allowed Ford to reuse engineering investments across multiple products and markets. The Fiesta and Focus became important examples of this approach.

The objective was to make Ford’s worldwide scale an operating advantage rather than a source of duplicated costs and conflicting priorities.

4. The Strategic Decisions That Saved Ford

Restructure Costs and Capacity

Ford had too many factories, workers, and costs for its declining market share.

By 2005, North American factories operated at only 79% of capacity. Ford was losing an average of $590 per vehicle manufactured in the region, while Toyota and Honda earned more than $1,200.

Mark Fields’ Way Forward restructuring plan provided a starting point. Mulally accelerated the restructuring while integrating it into One Ford.

The changes involved plant closures, workforce reductions, lower costs, and improved manufacturing efficiency.

Improve Products, Not Just Costs

Mulally believed Ford could not cut its way to sustainable success.

The company needed attractive, high-quality, fuel-efficient vehicles that customers wanted to buy.

  • Shared global platforms reduced duplicated engineering.
  • Improved quality addressed years of reputational damage.
  • Smaller cars and crossovers reduced dependence on large trucks and SUVs.
  • Technologies such as Ford SYNC helped differentiate products.
  • Better coordination improved product development and launch readiness.

The Edge launch delay demonstrated that quality problems needed to be resolved before vehicles reached customers.

Simplify the Business

Ford had accumulated a collection of premium brands that required management attention and capital.

The company sold Aston Martin, Jaguar, Land Rover, and eventually Volvo, concentrating resources on its core automotive operations.

Finance the Entire Turnaround

In late 2006, Ford secured approximately $23.5 billion in financing, pledging major assets—including the Blue Oval trademark—as collateral.

Don Leclair and Bill Ford played central roles in arranging and supporting the financing, with Mulally backing the decision.

The money provided capacity to absorb operating losses, restructure manufacturing, and continue investing in new products.

The decision proved critical when the financial crisis arrived in 2008 and credit markets froze.

Ford obtained financing before the worst of the crisis, while private lenders were still willing to provide it.

Restructure Labor Obligations

Ford negotiated with the United Auto Workers over costs, work rules, employment, and retiree benefits.

A major development was the agreement to transfer specified retiree healthcare obligations to a Voluntary Employee Beneficiary Association (VEBA) trust.

Further concessions became necessary as the financial crisis worsened.

The negotiations required management and labor to confront the gap between Ford’s historical obligations and its competitive financial position.

5. Running the System Through the 2008 Financial Crisis

By 2008, Ford had made operational progress, but collapsing vehicle demand threatened the company.

The management system remained intact even as the urgency increased.

How Ford Responded

  • More frequent reviews: Daily Special Attention Reviews supplemented the regular BPR during the crisis.
  • Revised forecasts: Financial and economic assumptions were updated as markets deteriorated.
  • Liquidity management: Cash preservation and financing capacity became immediate priorities.
  • Continued restructuring: Ford kept implementing the cost and organizational changes already underway.
  • Protected future products: Management sought to preserve investments needed for recovery.
  • Supplier-risk monitoring: Ford examined the possible collapse of GM and Chrysler because all three automakers depended on common suppliers.

Why Ford Avoided the Same Bailout as GM and Chrysler

GM and Chrysler entered bankruptcy in 2009 after requiring emergency government assistance.

Ford’s 2006 financing allowed it to continue without the same rescue loans or bankruptcy restructuring.

Ford nevertheless supported emergency assistance to the industry because the collapse of shared suppliers could disrupt its own production.

It also participated in other government financing programs, including a $5.9 billion Department of Energy loan for fuel-efficient vehicle development.

Ford’s survival reflected both operational restructuring and the financial cushion secured before the crisis.

6. Leadership, Results, and the Book’s Final Lessons

Mulally’s Leadership Approach

Hoffman portrays Mulally as combining engineering discipline, optimism, attention to detail, and a strong belief in collaborative execution.

  • Compelling vision: Establish a clear destination that employees and stakeholders can understand.
  • Comprehensive plan: Connect the company’s activities to a coordinated strategy.
  • Relentless implementation: Review progress continuously and address obstacles.
  • Facts and data: Understand the actual condition of the business rather than accept reassuring explanations.
  • Working together: Make collective success more important than executive territory.
  • Emotional resilience: Maintain confidence and discipline when circumstances deteriorate.
  • Respect for people: Listen, appreciate contributions, and avoid personal attacks while maintaining performance expectations.

Bill Ford’s Contribution

Bill Ford recognized the company’s problems, initiated restructuring, recruited Mulally, and helped secure support for major financial decisions.

He also gave Mulally the authority to run the company while providing continuity with the Ford family, board, and other stakeholders.

Hoffman presents the turnaround as a joint achievement rather than a rescue accomplished by Mulally alone.

Results

By 2010, Ford had substantially improved its operations and financial performance.

  • Ford reported $6.6 billion in net profit for 2010.
  • New products and improving quality strengthened its competitive position.
  • Global engineering and manufacturing became more integrated.
  • Restructuring reduced costs and excess capacity.
  • A common management process improved visibility and coordination.
  • Ford avoided the bankruptcy proceedings experienced by GM and Chrysler.

The book ends while Mulally is still CEO. Hoffman leaves open whether the cultural and managerial changes will endure after his departure.

Key Takeaways

  • The BPR was Mulally’s central management mechanism. Weekly, standardized reporting made the entire business visible and kept executives accountable.
  • The response to bad news changed behavior. Praising Mark Fields for exposing the Edge problem helped replace concealment with transparent reporting.
  • One Ford aligned the entire enterprise. Regional and functional teams stopped operating as independent competitors and increasingly shared objectives, engineering, and resources.
  • A simple plan was repeated relentlessly. Mulally maintained the same four strategic priorities instead of continually replacing them with new initiatives.
  • Collaboration was enforced through process. Working together became an explicit expectation of executive performance.
  • Product improvement and cost restructuring were inseparable. Ford needed better vehicles and an economically viable manufacturing system.
  • The 2006 financing preserved Ford’s options. Securing capital early gave the operational turnaround time to work through the 2008 crisis.
  • The lasting challenge was institutionalization. Ford’s recovery depended on whether the organization could sustain its new practices beyond Mulally’s personal leadership.

The central insight of American Icon: Mulally transformed Ford by giving its people a common plan, a shared view of reality, and a disciplined process for working together. Those management changes enabled the restructuring, product improvements, and financial decisions that brought the company back to profitability.
