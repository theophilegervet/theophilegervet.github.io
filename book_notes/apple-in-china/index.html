Apple in China

October 28, 2026

2026   ·   technology history  

Apple in China — Patrick McGee

Summary

Apple’s rise and China’s emergence as the world’s manufacturing superpower are deeply intertwined. Over two decades, Apple poured billions of dollars, sophisticated machinery, and extraordinary amounts of engineering talent into Chinese factories. It wasn’t simply outsourcing production to a country with cheap labor. It was helping build one of the most advanced electronics manufacturing ecosystems in the world.

The relationship began out of necessity. In the 1990s, Apple was nearly bankrupt and couldn’t afford its own factories. Tim Cook transformed the company into an asset-light operation, relying on Taiwanese contractors such as Foxconn that were rapidly expanding into mainland China. But Apple’s manufacturing demands were unlike anything its suppliers had encountered. Producing the iMac, iPod, and iPhone required new processes, equipment, and skills. Apple engineers spent extended periods inside factories helping suppliers meet specifications that previously seemed impossible.

This became the foundation of Apple’s competitive advantage. Apple controlled the design and captured the profits; Chinese factories and their suppliers accumulated manufacturing capabilities. Over time, the expertise Apple helped develop became available to a wider Chinese electronics industry, including companies that would eventually compete with it.

The relationship also created a political dependency. As Xi Jinping consolidated power, Beijing gained increasing leverage over a company that could neither easily abandon China’s factories nor afford to lose its consumers. Apple’s response involved enormous investment commitments, compliance with Chinese regulations, and careful management of its relationship with the Communist Party. McGee reveals a largely undisclosed 2016 agreement involving approximately $275 billion in China-related spending and investment commitments over five years.

The final chapters examine Apple’s attempts to reduce this dependency. COVID disruptions, geopolitical tensions, and the rise of Chinese competitors made diversification increasingly urgent. But Indian factories cannot simply replicate decades of accumulated manufacturing knowledge, and Apple’s dependence on Taiwan’s semiconductor industry presents a separate vulnerability.

The central argument: Apple’s manufacturing success in China was not simply a consequence of cheap labor. Apple helped develop the industrial capabilities that made China indispensable to its business. The same manufacturing system that enabled Apple’s extraordinary success has become one of its greatest strategic constraints.

1. Apple’s Retreat from Manufacturing (Chs. 1–4)

Apple’s eventual dependence on Asian manufacturing began with the near-collapse of its own manufacturing model in the mid-1990s. Outsourcing was initially a response to financial distress, not a grand strategy to build a China-centered production system.

  • Fountain, Colorado (1996): After Apple announced a quarterly loss of roughly $700 million, operations executive Joe O’Sullivan moved to sell its Macintosh factory in Fountain to contract manufacturer SCI Systems for approximately $200 million. Apple needed liquidity and relief from the costs of maintaining its own factories.

  • The abandonment of in-house production: Apple established an Outsourcing Group, eliminated thousands of jobs, and transferred manufacturing to contractors. Equipment from its Cork facility was loaded onto Boeing 747s and flown away. What had initially been presented as a balanced approach between internal and external manufacturing became an almost complete retreat from factory ownership.

  • Japan had shown the way: Apple already depended on Japanese manufacturers for technically demanding components and manufacturing work. These relationships demonstrated that outside specialists could possess capabilities difficult or expensive to reproduce internally.

  • Taiwan’s emerging competence: Apple’s Newton work with Inventec illustrated the initiative of Taiwanese suppliers. Inventec invested more than $800,000 in specialized equipment that was not previously available in its local industry. Suppliers were willing to acquire capabilities they didn’t yet possess to win Apple business.

  • The return of Steve Jobs (1997): Apple’s acquisition of NeXT brought Jobs back into the company. Gil Amelio had concentrated on repairing operations, but Apple still lacked a compelling product direction. Jobs saw a company suffering from a lack of focus and radically simplified its product portfolio.

  • Jobs’s manufacturing instincts: Jobs had a strong attachment to controlling how products were made. He did not immediately accept that Apple should surrender its factories. The tension between his desire for manufacturing control and the financial advantages of outsourcing shaped Apple’s emerging operating model.

Apple was abandoning factory ownership, but it wasn’t abandoning the ambition to control manufacturing. That distinction would become essential.

2. The iMac: Making the Impossible Manufacturable (Chs. 5–7)

The original iMac became Apple’s first great manufacturing test under Jobs. Its translucent, brightly colored enclosure was radically different from the beige computers of the era—and extraordinarily difficult to produce consistently.

  • The supposedly unmanufacturable iMac: Its translucent plastic enclosure required unusual precision in injection molding, material selection, color consistency, and surface finish. Imperfections that could be hidden inside an ordinary computer case were suddenly visible. Engineers had to solve production problems created directly by Apple’s industrial design.

  • Design was inseparable from manufacturing: Apple’s designers could specify shapes, materials, or finishes that factories couldn’t yet produce reliably at scale. Engineers then worked alongside suppliers to develop the necessary processes. The factory wasn’t simply executing a finished design; manufacturing itself had to evolve to make the design possible.

  • LG and the Asian financial crisis: South Korea’s economic distress created an opportunity for Apple to work with industrial firms eager for business. LG became an important manufacturing partner for the iMac.

  • A global production network: Apple and LG pursued production arrangements involving Wales and Mexico. Apple’s manufacturing geography was still unsettled. China had not yet become the default destination for nearly everything Apple built.

  • Success created an operations problem: The iMac revived Apple’s fortunes, but strong demand strained production capacity. A compelling product was commercially useful only if factories could manufacture enough units at the required quality, cost, and speed.

  • The recurring pattern: Apple demanded something factories couldn’t yet do, worked with suppliers to develop the necessary capabilities, and then expected reliable mass production. Each successful program left behind more sophisticated suppliers.

The iMac showed that outsourcing did not eliminate the need for manufacturing expertise inside Apple. In practice, Apple’s product ambitions required unusually close involvement in the factories it no longer owned.

3. Taiwan, Foxconn, and Tim Cook (Chs. 8–12)

China’s manufacturing rise was not independent of Taiwan. Taiwanese companies brought capital, technical expertise, international customers, and industrial management practices to the mainland, where they found expanding labor forces and supportive manufacturing infrastructure.

The Taiwanese Bridge to China

  • The taishang: Taiwanese entrepreneurs operating on the mainland helped connect China’s emerging industrial base to international electronics production. They brought manufacturing knowledge and customer relationships developed in Taiwan and established increasingly large production operations across the strait.

  • Terry Gou and Foxconn: Gou built Foxconn through aggressive expansion, cost discipline, and a willingness to undertake demanding manufacturing assignments. Where other suppliers might reject a project as too difficult, Foxconn saw an opportunity to acquire expertise and strengthen customer relationships.

  • China offered more than low wages: Manufacturing zones developed networks of factories, component suppliers, tooling specialists, logistics providers, and labor recruitment systems. The concentration of these capabilities became increasingly valuable as electronic products grew more complex.

  • Foxconn’s international experiments: Foxconn also established operations outside mainland China, including in California and the Czech Republic. Its eventual concentration in China emerged from repeated commercial and operational decisions rather than from an inevitable starting point.

Tim Cook Rebuilds Apple Operations

  • Cook joins Apple (1998): After working at IBM and Compaq, Cook brought systematic attention to inventory, sourcing, factory performance, and execution. Apple’s manufacturing practices had become expensive and inefficient; Cook helped replace them with a more disciplined outsourced-production model.

  • Inventory discipline: Computers lost value quickly as technology advanced. Warehouses filled with unsold products could become enormous financial liabilities. Cook pushed Apple toward an operating system that minimized inventory and relied on suppliers capable of responding quickly to demand.

  • Jobs’s “Mactories”: Jobs continued to entertain the possibility of Apple taking a larger direct manufacturing role, even distributing “Mactories” T-shirts. But the company’s operating model was moving decisively toward contractors.

  • Control without ownership: Apple could avoid owning factories while exercising deep control over what happened inside them. Its personnel determined specifications, negotiated costs, supervised engineering, and helped develop production processes. Contractors owned and operated the plants, but Apple shaped how its products were made.

The resulting model was not conventional hands-off outsourcing. Apple reduced its direct investment in factories while retaining an unusually active role in the manufacturing processes those factories used.

4. From iPod to iPhone: The Creation of an Industrial Ecosystem (Chs. 13–19)

The iPod and iPhone transformed Apple’s manufacturing relationships. Products became more sophisticated, production volumes exploded, and suppliers had to acquire capabilities that previously barely existed.

The iPod and Apple’s Supplier Power

  • The Taiwan-built iPod: Apple’s procurement organization, including Tony Blevins, negotiated demanding commercial arrangements with suppliers. The prospect of winning future Apple business gave Cupertino significant leverage to demand low prices, technical commitments, and manufacturing flexibility.

  • The iMac G4: Its distinctive flat-panel display and articulated arm created manufacturing difficulties across multiple components. Apple’s industrial designers continued to push suppliers beyond established production methods.

  • “China cost”: Apple increasingly expected suppliers to match the economics of production on the Chinese mainland. The phrase became a negotiating tool. Suppliers unable to deliver competitive pricing risked losing business to companies willing to move production or invest more aggressively.

  • Foxconn’s unsolicited iPod replica: Before receiving the contract, Foxconn built a replica of the iPod to demonstrate its manufacturing ability. It was an attempt to show Apple that Foxconn could master the product’s production requirements and deserved a role in its growing business.

  • Suppliers invested to win future contracts: Winning Apple business increasingly required acquiring equipment, technical expertise, and manufacturing capabilities before commercial returns were guaranteed. Apple could push suppliers into making expensive bets on future orders.

Project Purple and the iPhone

  • Project Purple: Developing the iPhone required coordinating Apple’s software, hardware, industrial design, and manufacturing teams with an extensive Asian supplier network. The finished product depended on many technically demanding components and processes working together.

  • The January 2007 unveiling: Jobs presented the iPhone as a phone, iPod, and internet communicator in one device. The simplicity of the public demonstration concealed the difficulty of building the product consistently at industrial scale.

  • A prototype is not a production line: Producing a few functional devices and manufacturing millions of identical units are fundamentally different challenges. Suppliers had to achieve acceptable yields, eliminate defects, and maintain precision at enormous volume.

  • Apple engineers inside factories: Apple personnel spent extended periods at supplier facilities solving production problems, refining machinery, improving tooling, and teaching manufacturing techniques. Expertise moved through direct engineering collaboration, not simply through specifications sent from California.

The “Apple Shock”

  • Scale forced industrial upgrading: Apple’s enormous orders and demanding specifications compelled suppliers to invest in equipment, skilled labor, process engineering, and factory expansion.

  • Factories became sites of technical instruction: Apple engineers worked directly with suppliers to develop new processes and improve existing ones. The relationship went far beyond awarding contracts to whichever factory submitted the lowest bid.

  • Knowledge remained after production: Equipment, techniques, and employee experience accumulated through Apple programs did not disappear when an individual product was finished. Suppliers could apply these capabilities to later projects and other customers.

  • Manufacturing expertise spread: Workers, engineers, component companies, and specialized suppliers became more experienced through participation in Apple’s production network. The effects extended beyond any single factory or product.

  • Apple’s impact went beyond purchasing: Through repeated product cycles, Apple helped develop the manufacturing capabilities that made China an increasingly sophisticated electronics production center.

Apple wasn’t simply using China’s existing industrial advantages. Through its demanding product programs, engineering involvement, and enormous production volumes, it was helping expand those advantages.

5. Apple Discovers the Chinese Consumer Market (Chs. 20–25)

Apple had spent years establishing intimate relationships with Chinese factories, yet understood surprisingly little about selling products directly to Chinese consumers. Its manufacturing operations and retail organization operated in very different worlds.

  • John Ford arrives in Beijing: Ford was tasked with establishing Apple’s retail operations in China. Despite Apple’s enormous manufacturing presence, the retail organization lacked much of the local infrastructure and institutional knowledge it needed.

  • The sewing-machine repair shop: Licensing requirements complicated the opening of Apple’s first Beijing retail store. The team worked around the problem by acquiring an existing small business, illustrating the practical difficulties foreign companies encountered navigating Chinese bureaucracy.

  • The “yellow cows”: Scalpers and organized resellers exploited demand for Apple products, purchasing devices and reselling them at higher prices. Product launches attracted crowds, security problems, and gray-market trading.

  • Apple’s retail model meets China: Practices developed in the United States produced unexpected difficulties in China. Store operations had to contend with local regulations, contractors, consumer behavior, and security arrangements.

  • Jobs’s intervention: Negative coverage of Apple’s Chinese retail operations could provoke direct intervention from Jobs. Problems that appeared local became urgent concerns at the highest levels of Apple.

  • Security and contractor problems: Ford’s experiences exposed the difficulties of running prominent retail stores where valuable inventory, large crowds, third-party security personnel, and local authorities intersected.

  • Manufacturing familiarity was not market familiarity: Apple’s deep involvement in Chinese production had not prepared its retail teams to navigate the country’s consumer culture and institutional environment.

Apple knew how to manufacture products in China long before it understood how to operate as a consumer-facing company there.

6. Foxconn, TSMC, and Two Forms of Dependence (Ch. 24; Ch. 41)

Apple’s increasing reliance on external production concentrated critical manufacturing capabilities in two very different kinds of companies: Foxconn in electronics assembly and TSMC in advanced semiconductors.

  • Foxconn’s manufacturing scale: Foxconn developed the capacity to assemble enormous volumes of sophisticated electronics, coordinating labor, components, machinery, and production schedules at a scale few competitors could match.

  • TSMC’s semiconductor expertise: Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company became indispensable to the production of increasingly advanced chips. Its capabilities resulted from decades of specialization, capital investment, and technical development.

  • Apple-designed processors: Designing its own chips gave Apple greater control over product performance and the integration of hardware and software. But chip design did not provide the ability to manufacture those chips. Apple remained dependent on specialized semiconductor foundries.

  • Different production challenges: Final assembly and semiconductor fabrication involve fundamentally different manufacturing capabilities. Moving assembly lines to another country would not solve the problem of replicating leading-edge chip production.

  • The 2008 financial crisis: McGee describes how the crisis altered perceptions of American economic leadership and contributed to China’s growing confidence. The political environment was changing even as Apple became more deeply embedded in Asian manufacturing.

  • Concentration in two geographies: Mainland China became essential to electronics production, while Taiwan remained essential to advanced chip fabrication. These dependencies exposed Apple to distinct political and operational risks.

Apple had developed extraordinary control over product design while depending on manufacturing capabilities concentrated in places it did not control politically.

7. Xi Jinping Changes the Rules (Chs. 26–30)

For much of Apple’s expansion, the prevailing assumption was that deeper economic integration would make China more commercially open. Xi Jinping’s rise exposed the weakness of that assumption.

  • Xi takes power (2012): Xi became general secretary of the Chinese Communist Party in November 2012. His consolidation of political authority changed the environment facing foreign companies operating in China.

  • The 2013 state-media campaign: Chinese state media attacked Apple over its customer-service and warranty practices. The coordinated criticism demonstrated the government’s ability to turn a commercial dispute into an issue of national political significance.

  • The “Gang of Eight”: McGee recounts the people and institutions involved in the pressure on Apple. The episode illustrated how the company’s reputation and position in the Chinese market could be subjected to politically directed scrutiny.

  • Doug Guthrie’s warning: Guthrie, a scholar of China who joined Apple, sought to explain the country’s political institutions and changing economic direction to company leadership. His experience revealed the difficulty of translating knowledge of Chinese politics into decisions at Cupertino.

  • “Voluntary is the new mandatory”: As the government became more assertive, requests framed as voluntary cooperation increasingly carried implicit pressure. A foreign company might theoretically refuse, but refusal could threaten its access to the market or ability to operate.

  • The limits of Apple’s power: Apple could impose extraordinary demands on suppliers. But in dealings with the Chinese government, the balance of leverage was fundamentally different.

  • The China squeeze: Apple’s factories, suppliers, employees, and consumer business were so deeply embedded in China that political friction carried enormous commercial risks.

Apple had become powerful enough to dictate terms to factories, but too dependent on China to dictate terms to Beijing.

8. The $275 Billion Agreement and Apple’s Accommodation with Beijing (Chs. 31–32)

One of McGee’s most important revelations concerns Apple’s largely undisclosed negotiations with the Chinese government in 2016. As Beijing increased pressure, Apple pursued an agreement intended to protect its position in the country.

The “Marshall Plan for China”

  • The 2016 agreement: McGee reports that Apple committed to approximately $275 billion in China-related investment and spending over five years. The arrangement was intended to align Apple’s activities with Chinese economic and industrial priorities while protecting its ability to operate in the country.

  • Not a single $275 billion payment: The agreement covered a broad range of economic activity and commitments. The headline figure should not be interpreted as one payment or as expenditure entirely separate from Apple’s existing Chinese operations.

  • What Beijing wanted: China sought assurances that Apple’s presence would contribute to domestic industrial development, supplier advancement, and government priorities. The economic benefits of Apple’s operations had become a matter of political negotiation.

  • What Apple needed: Apple wanted to preserve access to China’s manufacturing system and consumer market. With so much of its business dependent on the country, maintaining a workable relationship with Beijing was essential.

  • The secrecy of the arrangement: The scale of the commitments contrasted with the limited public disclosure surrounding the negotiations.

Didi, Digital Services, and Data

  • Apple invests in Didi (2016): Apple made a $1 billion investment in Chinese ride-hailing company Didi Chuxing. McGee places the investment within a broader effort to strengthen Apple’s economic and political relationships in China.

  • iTunes and iBooks shut down: Chinese regulators closed Apple’s iTunes Movies and iBooks services shortly before Didi president Jean Liu visited Cupertino. Even Apple’s substantial investments did not shield its digital services from government intervention.

  • Chinese iCloud infrastructure: Apple developed arrangements to store Chinese users’ iCloud data inside China, including in Guizhou. These arrangements raised difficult questions about how Apple balanced local legal requirements with its global commitments to privacy and security.

  • The Chen Min’er miscalculation: Some within Apple’s orbit believed Chen Min’er, a senior official associated with Guizhou, might eventually rise to China’s top leadership. The Communist Party’s 2017 leadership decisions instead reinforced Xi’s consolidation of power.

  • Political relationships were difficult to predict: Apple’s efforts to build relationships with influential figures and institutions faced the uncertainties of Chinese elite politics. Commercial access depended on political developments that Apple could neither direct nor reliably anticipate.

The relationship had changed. Apple was no longer dealing with China solely as a place to manufacture and sell products. Its continued success increasingly required accommodation with a government whose priorities could override ordinary commercial considerations.

9. The Human Cost of Apple’s Manufacturing System (Ch. 33)

Apple imposed rigorous quality requirements on suppliers, yet ensuring comparable standards for workers proved much harder. McGee examines the gap between Apple’s public supplier-responsibility commitments and the conditions documented inside its factories.

  • Jacky Haynes and Supplier Responsibility: Haynes led efforts to improve labor conditions at Apple’s suppliers. McGee portrays her as serious about reform while documenting the limits of what the organization could accomplish.

  • The 2013 Pegatron investigation: China Labor Watch reported problems at a Shanghai factory including underage and student labor, excessive overtime, crowded dormitories, and insufficient rest. Haynes interrupted an audit at a Foxconn facility in Chengdu to investigate.

  • Product defects versus labor violations: Labor activists argued that Apple responded with far greater speed and force to problems affecting product quality than to violations affecting workers. The criticism exposed a tension between Apple’s operational priorities and its stated ethical standards.

  • The limits of factory audits: Suppliers could prepare facilities for inspection and manipulate the conditions auditors encountered. McGee describes methods used to conceal underage workers, including factory music serving as a signal for certain workers to leave before inspectors arrived.

  • Conflicting supplier incentives: Apple demanded low prices, high yields, fast turnaround, and extraordinary production flexibility. Suppliers faced pressure to satisfy these commercial requirements while complying with labor standards. Those objectives could come into conflict.

  • Independent scrutiny became harder: As the Chinese government tightened restrictions on civil society organizations, outside labor monitoring and collaboration became increasingly difficult. This undermined an important source of accountability.

  • Reform efforts versus structural problems: The book reports sustained efforts by individuals inside Apple to improve conditions. But it also questions whether those efforts could overcome the incentives and constraints built into the wider production system.

The contradiction was particularly sharp because Apple had already demonstrated how deeply it could intervene in suppliers’ operations when product quality was at stake.

10. China’s Suppliers Become Competitors (Chs. 34–37)

The same industrial system that helped Apple dominate consumer electronics was producing increasingly sophisticated Chinese manufacturers. Mainland companies began taking work previously dominated by Taiwanese suppliers, while Chinese smartphone brands challenged Apple directly.

The Red Supply Chain

  • Isabel Ge Mahe (2017): Apple appointed Mahe managing director of Greater China. Although the title appeared to signal substantial regional authority, McGee reports that her actual influence was much more constrained than outsiders might assume.

  • The rise of mainland suppliers: Chinese manufacturers increasingly secured important positions within Apple’s production network. The historical dominance of Taiwanese companies was challenged by businesses that had accumulated experience and expanded their technical capabilities.

  • Luxshare: Luxshare illustrates this progression. The company developed increasingly sophisticated manufacturing capabilities and won important Apple business, including production of wearable devices.

  • The “red supply chain”: As Chinese-owned suppliers gained share, more of the expertise, production capacity, and commercial value associated with Apple’s products accumulated inside mainland Chinese companies.

  • Capabilities outlast contracts: Factories retained the workers, machinery, engineering knowledge, and management experience developed through Apple programs. These assets could support future Apple products or other customers.

  • The supplier relationship evolved: Apple had originally relied heavily on Taiwanese intermediaries to connect it to Chinese production. The rise of mainland suppliers meant that Chinese companies increasingly possessed the expertise to compete for sophisticated Apple work directly.

Huawei and the Smartphone Competition

  • The iPhone XR disappointment (2018): Weak demand and deteriorating sales expectations triggered alarm within Apple. McGee describes the urgency surrounding the company’s worsening commercial outlook.

  • The revenue warning: Apple’s warning after the 2018 holiday quarter produced a severe investor reaction. It exposed the extent to which expectations for Apple’s performance depended on continued iPhone growth and success in China.

  • Huawei’s rise: Huawei became a formidable competitor in the Chinese smartphone market. Chinese manufacturers were no longer confined to inexpensive, low-end devices. They were developing sophisticated products and competing more directly with Apple’s offerings.

  • Industrial strength supported domestic competition: China’s increasingly capable electronics industry supported a broader ecosystem of smartphone manufacturers and component suppliers. McGee connects this transformation to the industrial capabilities accumulated over decades, while also examining factors specific to individual Chinese competitors.

  • China’s multiple roles: Apple depended on China as a manufacturing base, relied on its consumers for revenue, and faced increasingly powerful competition from its domestic technology industry.

China had become three things to Apple at once: its most important manufacturing ecosystem, a major consumer market, and home to increasingly formidable competitors.

11. COVID, India, and the Difficulty of Leaving China (Chs. 38–41)

The COVID pandemic exposed how concentrated Apple’s manufacturing system had become. Years of prioritizing manufacturing efficiency and supplier capability had left the company vulnerable to disruptions and political decisions beyond its control.

COVID Exposes the Dependency

  • Wuhan lockdown (January 2020): China’s response to the outbreak disrupted travel, labor movement, and factory operations. Apple had to adapt to rapidly changing restrictions affecting its suppliers and manufacturing network.

  • Zero-COVID: The government’s strict controls gave public-health decisions immediate consequences for production. Factories depended on the movement of workers and components across an interconnected supply chain.

  • Zhengzhou: Disruptions and worker protests at Foxconn’s enormous iPhone manufacturing complex became one of the clearest examples of the vulnerability. Government restrictions, labor conditions, and concentrated production capacity combined into a serious manufacturing crisis.

  • The cost of concentration: Apple’s manufacturing system relied on dense networks of suppliers, workers, and factories. Disruption at one major production center could affect output far beyond that individual facility.

  • Government policy became a manufacturing variable: Decisions made by Chinese authorities directly affected Apple’s ability to produce and ship products. The company couldn’t resolve these problems solely through better factory management.

India as “Plan B”

  • The push into India: Apple placed greater emphasis on Indian iPhone assembly to reduce its concentration in China. But expanding assembly capacity proved considerably harder than simply opening another factory.

  • A factory is not a supply chain: Manufacturing an iPhone requires specialized components, machinery, experienced engineers, quality-control systems, logistics, and suppliers capable of solving difficult problems quickly. Much of this infrastructure had taken decades to develop in China.

  • Manufacturing expertise is difficult to relocate: China’s advantage rested partly on the concentration of people who already knew how to manufacture complex electronics at enormous scale. That accumulated knowledge could not simply be transferred by moving equipment.

  • The surrounding ecosystem matters: An assembly plant depends on a network of nearby suppliers, technicians, component specialists, and logistics providers. If essential parts or expertise must repeatedly come from elsewhere, production becomes harder to coordinate.

  • Restrictions on equipment and personnel: McGee describes China’s efforts to constrain or complicate the movement of machinery and technical expertise to India. Diversification was vulnerable to political obstacles as well as operational ones.

  • India cannot instantly replace China: Growing Indian production could reduce Apple’s dependence over time, but matching China’s scale and manufacturing sophistication would require sustained investment and accumulated experience.

Taiwan Remains a Separate Vulnerability

  • TSMC’s central role: Even successful diversification of final assembly would leave Apple dependent on advanced semiconductor manufacturing in Taiwan.

  • Chip fabrication is exceptionally difficult to replicate: Leading-edge semiconductor production requires enormous capital investment, accumulated engineering expertise, and highly specialized manufacturing systems. Moving it is a substantially different challenge from relocating electronics assembly.

  • Cross-strait tensions: The concentration of advanced chip production in Taiwan creates a major geopolitical exposure for Apple, independent of its assembly operations in mainland China.

  • Diversification cannot erase decades of dependence: Apple’s manufacturing network was built through thousands of decisions, years of engineering collaboration, supplier investment, and accumulated industrial knowledge. Reproducing those capabilities elsewhere is a long process.

The book ends with a difficult reality: Apple cannot quickly unwind the manufacturing system it spent decades helping build. China’s advantage is not simply its factories or labor costs, but the accumulated expertise, equipment, suppliers, and industrial coordination embedded across the country. Moving assembly elsewhere requires rebuilding much of that capability, while advanced semiconductor production leaves Apple dependent on Taiwan regardless of where its phones are assembled.
