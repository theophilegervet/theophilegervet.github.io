*Michael Porter, Competitive Strategy + Joan Magretta, Understanding Michael Porter*

## 1. What Strategy Actually Is

**Strategy is a set of choices that allows a company to create distinctive value and earn superior returns despite competition.**

It must answer three questions:

1. **Where to compete:** Which customers, needs, and markets to serve?
2. **How to win:** What distinctive value will the company provide, and through which activities?
3. **Why the advantage will last:** What makes the position difficult or costly to imitate?

A goal ("become the market leader"), an initiative ("launch new products"), or a capability ("hire great engineers") is not a strategy. None explains why the business will be more profitable than its competitors.

### Compete to Be Unique, Not the Best

There is rarely one universally best product because customers have different needs. Trying to be the best encourages companies to imitate each other, eventually making price the main basis of competition.

**Example — Walmart vs. Target:** Walmart competes through everyday low prices; Target combines attractive prices with style. Both can succeed because they serve different preferences.

The goal is not to beat every competitor on every dimension. It is to **serve chosen customers in a way competitors do not match**.

## 2. The Economics of Competitive Advantage

A company can outperform competitors in only two fundamental ways:

- **Higher relative price:** Offer differentiated value that customers will pay more for.
- **Lower relative cost:** Deliver comparable value through a less costly activity system.

Both can coexist. What matters is the resulting spread between price and cost.

### Differentiation

Differentiation matters only when customers value it enough to pay a premium exceeding its additional cost.

The value need not be a product feature. It may be reliability, convenience, service, brand, reduced risk, or lower total cost to the customer.

**Example — Caterpillar:** Durable equipment, available replacement parts, and a strong dealer network reduce customer downtime. These benefits can justify higher prices.

### Cost Advantage

Cost advantage comes from the full system of activities, not simply cheaper manufacturing or lower wages.

**Example — Dell:** Historically, direct selling, build-to-order production, outsourced components, and low inventory produced lower costs and working-capital requirements than traditional PC manufacturers.

### Measure the Right Outcome

Porter favors **long-term return on invested capital (ROIC)** because it considers both profit and the resources required to produce it.

Revenue growth, market share, and margins are incomplete measures. A company can increase all three without improving its economic returns.

**Example — Nucor:** Its roughly 18% ROIC was exceptional relative to the steel industry's approximately 6% average, whereas Pharmacia & Upjohn's 19.6% lagged stronger pharmaceutical competitors. Relative performance matters more than impressive numbers in isolation.

## 3. The Five Forces: Who Captures Industry Profits?

**An industry can create enormous value while its companies capture very little of it.** The five forces explain how competition distributes that value.

| Force | Core question | Effect on profits |
|---|---|---|
| **Rivalry** | How aggressively do existing competitors compete? | Lower prices or higher competitive spending |
| **Buyer power** | How much leverage do customers have? | Lower prices or more service for the same price |
| **Supplier power** | How dependent are companies on input providers? | Higher input costs or worse terms |
| **Substitutes** | Can customers solve the same need differently? | Limits how much the industry can charge |
| **New entrants** | How easily can new competitors enter? | Constrains prices and encourages defensive investment |

### 3.1 Rivalry — Competition Among Existing Firms

Rivalry destroys profitability when companies compete away the value they create, particularly through price cuts.

Rivalry intensifies when:

- **Products are similar:** Customers choose primarily on price.
- **Switching costs are low:** Buyers can change suppliers easily.
- **Growth is slow:** Companies must steal share to expand.
- **Fixed costs are high:** Firms discount to utilize capacity.
- **Capacity is perishable:** Unsold inventory or service capacity loses its value.
- **Exit barriers are high:** Unprofitable firms remain and continue competing.

**Example — Airlines:** Flights have high fixed costs, while an additional passenger costs relatively little. Unsold seats become worthless at departure, encouraging discounting. Combined with easy price comparison, this makes airline competition particularly destructive.

**Insight:** Rivalry is most dangerous when competitors offer similar value to the same customers and compete primarily on price.

### 3.2 Buyer Power — Customers Capture the Value

Powerful buyers negotiate lower prices or demand more value without paying proportionately more.

Buyer power increases when:

- Few buyers account for substantial sales.
- Products are standardized and easily compared.
- Buyers can switch suppliers cheaply.
- Buyers have credible alternatives or can produce the product themselves.

**Buyer power differs from price sensitivity.** Power is the ability to negotiate; sensitivity is the motivation. Customers care more about price when a purchase is expensive relative to their budget or has little effect on their own performance.

**Example — Cement:** Large U.S. construction companies can negotiate aggressively with cement suppliers. In Mexico, numerous small purchasers historically had much less leverage against concentrated cement producers. CEMEX consequently earned higher returns in Mexico.

**Insight:** The same product can have very different profitability depending on who buys it.

### 3.3 Supplier Power — Input Providers Capture the Value

Suppliers can raise prices or impose unfavorable terms when companies depend on inputs that are difficult to replace.

Supplier power increases when suppliers are concentrated, their inputs are essential or differentiated, switching is costly, or they have attractive alternative customers.

**Example — Personal computers:** Microsoft and Intel historically controlled essential, differentiated components. PC manufacturers depended on them while competing aggressively with one another, allowing the suppliers to capture substantial value.

**Insight:** Owning the customer relationship does not guarantee attractive profits when powerful suppliers control essential inputs.

### 3.4 Substitutes — Different Ways to Solve the Same Problem

A substitute satisfies the underlying customer need through a different approach, placing a ceiling on what an industry can charge.

The threat depends on the alternative's **total price–performance value**, not price alone.

**Example — High-speed rail:** The Madrid–Barcelona train offered city-center travel, comfort, and fewer airport inconveniences. It attracted passengers even when tickets cost more than low-cost flights. The relevant competition was between ways of making the journey, not merely between airlines.

**Insight:** Competitors selling similar products are only part of the threat. Alternatives solving the same need can be more consequential.

### 3.5 New Entrants — Potential Competition Limits Returns

Profitable industries attract entrants. Even before entry occurs, incumbents may limit prices or invest more heavily to discourage newcomers.

Important **barriers to entry** include:

- **Economies of scale:** Large firms enjoy lower unit costs.
- **Network effects:** More users make an offering more valuable.
- **Switching costs:** Customers face difficulty changing providers.
- **Capital requirements:** Entry demands substantial investment.
- **Incumbent advantages:** Brands, proprietary technology, learning, or favorable locations.
- **Distribution access:** Established firms control important channels.
- **Regulation:** Licensing or policy restricts entry.
- **Expected retaliation:** Entrants anticipate aggressive competitive responses.

**Example — Starbucks:** Specialty coffee retailing has relatively low entry barriers, requiring Starbucks to keep improving its offering to prevent competitors from exploiting neglected opportunities.

**Insight:** High profits attract competition unless something prevents entrants from reproducing the economics.

### Putting the Forces Together

**Example — The historical PC industry:** Powerful suppliers (Microsoft and Intel), increasingly price-sensitive buyers, intense manufacturer rivalry, and emerging substitutes constrained manufacturers' profitability. Apple, however, used its own operating system and differentiated products to occupy a more favorable position.

This reveals the two levels of strategy:

- **Industry structure determines the average profit potential.**
- **Competitive positioning determines whether a company can outperform that average.**

Five-forces analysis is not merely about declaring an industry attractive or unattractive. It identifies **which economic pressures matter most, how they are changing, and where a company can position itself favorably**.

## 4. The Three Generic Strategies

Porter identifies three broad routes to advantage:

| Strategy | How it works | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost leadership** | Achieve a lower cost structure across a broad competitive scope. | Imitation, technological change, or neglected customer needs |
| **Differentiation** | Create valued differences supporting a premium. | Premium exceeds customer value or differentiation costs too much |
| **Focus** | Serve a narrow segment especially well through cost advantage or differentiation. | Broad rivals or narrower specialists erode the advantage |

**Example — Martin-Brower:** By specializing in distribution to major fast-food chains, it could tailor warehouses, ordering, and product assortments to their requirements rather than serve the entire food-distribution market.

### Stuck in the Middle

This means **having no coherent source of competitive advantage**, often because the company combines incompatible activities or promises.

It does not mean a company must always choose between low cost and differentiation. A tailored system can improve selected customer benefits while reducing costs.

## 5. The Five Tests of a Good Strategy

Magretta brings Porter's ideas together through five tests.

### Test 1: Distinctive Value Proposition

A strategy must specify:

1. **Which customers?**
2. **Which needs?**
3. **What relative price?**

The goal is to choose a particular kind of value, rather than trying to satisfy every customer equally.

**Example — Enterprise Rent-A-Car:** Enterprise historically focused on local customers needing temporary replacement vehicles rather than primarily airport travelers. Those customers valued neighborhood convenience and reasonable prices.

### Test 2: Tailored Value Chain

**Competitive advantage comes from the activities performed and how they are configured.**

A value chain includes designing, sourcing, producing, selling, delivering, and supporting an offering. The activities should be specifically suited to the chosen value proposition.

**Example — Enterprise:** Neighborhood locations, relationships with insurers and dealerships, and suitable fleet policies supported local replacement rentals. Its advantage came from an operating system different from airport-focused rivals.

A value proposition without distinctive supporting activities is usually easy to imitate.

### Test 3: Tradeoffs

**Strategy requires choosing what not to do.** Serving one position well may be incompatible with serving another.

Tradeoffs arise because different positions require different activities, reputations, or organizational priorities.

**Example — TSMC:** It chose to manufacture semiconductors without designing chips that competed with its customers. Forgoing the design business made its neutrality credible and supported manufacturing specialization.

Tradeoffs protect against imitation because rivals cannot copy a position without sacrificing or restructuring parts of their existing business.

**Straddling** occurs when a company tries to serve incompatible positions simultaneously, often inheriting both systems' costs without achieving either position effectively.

### Test 4: Fit

**Activities create more advantage when they reinforce one another.**

Magretta describes three forms:

- **Consistency:** Activities support the same positioning.
- **Reinforcement:** One activity improves the effectiveness of another.
- **Substitution:** One activity eliminates or reduces the need for another.

**Example — IKEA:** Flat-pack furniture lowers storage and transport costs; customers transport and assemble purchases; large stores combine displays with self-service warehouses. Each choice strengthens the others.

**Example — Zara:** Rapid design, frequent information from stores, nearby production for some garments, small batches, and fast distribution supported frequent new merchandise and fewer markdowns. Some individual activities cost more, but the overall system performed better.

**Insight:** A strategy is harder to imitate when advantage comes from an interconnected system rather than one feature or capability.

### Test 5: Continuity

**A strategy needs time to accumulate its advantages.** Reputation, specialized skills, customer relationships, and activity fit improve through sustained commitment to a position.

Continuity does not mean refusing innovation. It means improving how the position is delivered without repeatedly abandoning its underlying choices.

**Example — Southwest:** Rapid aircraft turnarounds emerged while maintaining its schedule with fewer aircraft. The operational discovery reinforced its low-cost, convenient-service position.

Fundamental change is warranted when customer needs disappear, technology eliminates essential advantages, or the economic logic of the existing position breaks down.

## 6. Competitor Analysis: Anticipate Responses

A strategic move's success depends partly on how rivals respond. Porter analyzes competitors through four dimensions:

| Dimension | What to understand |
|---|---|
| **Future goals** | What the rival wants and what it will defend |
| **Assumptions** | What it believes about itself, customers, and the industry |
| **Current strategy** | How it currently competes |
| **Capabilities** | What it can realistically do and sustain |

Together these reveal likely moves, vulnerabilities, incentives to retaliate, and limitations on effective responses.

**Example — IBM and minicomputers:** Responding aggressively to minicomputers threatened IBM's established mainframe business. Its difficulty involved conflicting economic incentives, not just technological capability.

### Market Signals and Commitment

Announcements, investments, pricing changes, and public statements can reveal competitors' intentions, but may also be attempts to influence expectations. Actions supported by costly commitments tend to be more credible than unsupported claims.

**Commitment** can deter competitors by demonstrating a willingness to persist, but can also create rigidity or provoke damaging retaliation.

The key is to evaluate a move **after accounting for competitors' likely responses**, not only its immediate benefit.

## 7. Strategic Groups and Mobility Barriers

**Strategic groups are companies within an industry pursuing similar competitive positions.** They may differ by specialization, quality, product range, distribution, integration, geography, and cost structure.

**Mobility barriers** are obstacles preventing companies from moving easily between those positions. They include reputation, specialized assets, scale, distribution relationships, accumulated expertise, and organizational requirements.

**Example — Major appliances:** Porter distinguishes broad-line integrated manufacturers, premium specialists, and private-label producers. Each group has different economics and requirements; shifting between them can demand substantial investments and organizational changes.

This explains why companies in the same industry can have different profitability and why rivals cannot simply adopt whichever position earns the highest returns.

An unoccupied position is not automatically an opportunity. It may lack demand or require an economically unattractive configuration.

## 8. Industry Evolution

**Industry structure changes as customer needs, technology, input costs, learning, regulation, and competitive behavior evolve.**

Porter warns against treating industry development as a predictable product life cycle. What matters is how changes affect the five forces and the sources of competitive advantage.

### Fragmented Industries

Fragmentation often persists because of local needs, customization, personal service, transport costs, or limited economies of scale.

**Example — Prelude:** Its attempt to industrialize lobster fishing through expensive vessels and facilities failed to create sufficient cost advantages. Scale did not overcome the industry's structural constraints.

The lesson is to identify why fragmentation exists before assuming consolidation will improve economics.

### Emerging Industries

Emerging industries have uncertainty about technology, customers, standards, distribution, and future competitors. Pioneers may need to educate customers or build complementary infrastructure.

**Example — Corning:** It invested in connectors, splicing, and light sources to support the emerging fiber-optic industry despite focusing its own business on fiber and cable.

First-mover advantage exists only when early entry creates durable assets such as relationships, proprietary learning, or access. Pioneers can instead bear costs that later entrants avoid.

### Maturing Industries

Slowing growth increases battles for market share, buyer sophistication, cost pressure, and the danger of excess capacity. Companies may need to rationalize products, improve processes, and become more selective about customers and investment.

### Declining Industries

Decline can be manageable or destructive depending on demand patterns, remaining niches, and exit barriers.

Porter identifies four alternatives:

- **Leadership:** Become a leading remaining competitor.
- **Niche:** Concentrate on an attractive surviving segment.
- **Harvest:** Reduce investment and generate cash.
- **Divest:** Exit before value deteriorates further.

### Global Industries

Global competition matters when competitive positions across countries are economically interconnected.

Scale, shared research, coordinated activities, and favorable production locations can create advantages. But transport costs, regulation, local preferences, and service requirements may limit them.

International expansion is valuable only when the economic benefits exceed the costs of adapting and coordinating across markets.

## 9. Buyer Selection and Purchasing Strategy

**Not all customers are equally attractive.** Porter evaluates buyers along four dimensions:

1. Fit between their needs and the company's capabilities.
2. Potential for future growth.
3. Bargaining power and price sensitivity.
4. Full cost of selling to and serving them.

A large customer can be unattractive if it demands excessive discounts, customization, or support. A demanding customer can still be valuable if it pays adequately for distinctive capabilities.

**Purchasing strategy** similarly requires balancing input prices against long-term supplier dependence. Using several suppliers, developing alternatives, standardizing inputs, or producing some inputs internally can improve bargaining power, but may sacrifice discounts or operating efficiencies.

The correct objective is favorable long-run economics, not automatically the largest customer or lowest purchasing price.

## 10. Vertical Integration

**Vertical integration brings supplier or distribution activities inside the business.** It can improve coordination, reliability, differentiation, access, and bargaining position, but also requires capital and creates fixed costs, management complexity, and reduced flexibility.

The decision should compare the full economics of ownership with alternatives:

- **Contracts:** Coordinate activities while retaining independent companies.
- **Tapered integration:** Perform some activities internally while continuing to buy or sell externally.
- **Quasi-integration:** Create closer relationships through partial ownership, financing, or cooperative arrangements.

A supplier's margin is not automatically available as savings from integration because the company must take on the supplier's costs and risks.

**Insight:** Own an adjacent activity when integration improves the complete system's economics, not merely because the activity is profitable on its own.

---

## 11. Capacity Expansion

**Capacity decisions depend on what competitors build, not just the company's own demand forecast.**

When firms independently forecast growth and expand, total industry capacity may exceed demand. High fixed costs then encourage discounting to fill capacity, eroding returns.

The risk increases when capacity arrives in large increments, projects take years to finish, forecasts are optimistic, and exit barriers are high.

**Preemptive expansion** seeks to deter rivals by committing capacity first. It works only if the commitment is credible, economically significant, and rivals choose not to match it. Otherwise, it can trigger overinvestment and destructive competition.

---

## 12. Entry Into New Businesses

**An attractive industry is not necessarily attractive to enter.** Incumbents may earn strong returns because entry barriers protect them; overcoming those barriers can consume the returns available to newcomers.

Entry must account for capital requirements, distribution access, reputation building, early losses, learning, and retaliation.

Porter distinguishes:

- **Internal development:** Build the business and overcome barriers directly.
- **Acquisition:** Buy an existing position, recognizing that the purchase price may already reflect future profits.
- **Sequenced entry:** Enter through one position and later move into another as capabilities accumulate.

**Example — P&G and Charmin:** P&G acquired a regional producer and subsequently invested in product development, manufacturing, national distribution, and advertising to establish a broader competitive position.

The central question is whether the entrant can establish an economically superior position **after all entry costs and competitive responses are considered**.

## 13. Growth, Innovation, and Leadership

### Growth Should Reinforce Strategy

Pressure to grow can encourage companies to serve incompatible customers, add excessive product complexity, or enter businesses where they have no advantage.

Porter favors deepening a strong position before expanding beyond it: reach more compatible customers, improve the activities serving them, and extend into markets where the proposition remains economically coherent.

A profitable business need not pursue unlimited growth. Expanding at the expense of returns can destroy value.

### Innovation Should Strengthen the Economics

Innovation can improve operational effectiveness, reinforce the existing activity system, or fundamentally change an industry's structure.

Not every new technology requires a new strategy. The important question is whether it changes customer value, relative costs, tradeoffs, or competitive forces.

### Leadership Maintains Coherence

Departments naturally optimize their own operations, but strategy requires coordinating the entire system.

Leadership must define the position, make tradeoffs, align activities and incentives, allocate resources consistently, and recognize when structural change requires adaptation.

A strategy cannot be assembled simply by combining separate departmental plans or annual budgets.

## 14. The Complete Strategic Logic

All the frameworks connect into one explanation:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| **Industry structure** | How much profit can companies in this industry retain? |
| **Competitive position** | Which customers and needs can this company serve distinctively? |
| **Competitive advantage** | How do its activities produce better relative prices or costs? |
| **Tradeoffs** | What must it forgo to preserve that advantage? |
| **Fit** | How do the activities reinforce one another? |
| **Sustainability** | Why is the system difficult to imitate? |
| **Continuity** | How does the advantage deepen while conditions evolve? |

### The Most Important Mistakes

- Confusing goals, growth, or operational efficiency with strategy.
- Trying to be universally the best instead of distinctively valuable.
- Assuming market share automatically creates profitability.
- Looking only at direct rivals rather than all five forces.
- Treating customer value as proof that the business captures value.
- Copying competitors' features without their supporting activities.
- Refusing to make tradeoffs or serving incompatible customers.
- Optimizing individual functions at the expense of the whole system.
- Entering attractive industries without accounting for entry costs.
- Pursuing growth that weakens the original position.
- Changing strategy too frequently—or preserving it after its economic foundation disappears.

## The Essence

**Strategy is about making choices that create a different and economically superior way of competing.**

The five forces explain the industry's underlying profit potential. Positioning determines the customers and needs a company chooses to serve. A tailored value chain turns that proposition into superior relative prices or costs. Tradeoffs protect the position from imitation; fit makes its activities mutually reinforcing; continuity allows the advantage to accumulate.

The strongest strategies do not depend on being better at everything. They depend on **being distinctively valuable to chosen customers through a coherent system of activities that rivals cannot easily reproduce without compromising their own positions.**
