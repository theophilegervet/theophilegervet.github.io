# The Prize: The Epic Quest for Oil, Money, and Power
Daniel Yergin (1991)

# Introduction: Oil, Money, and Power

Oil became the most strategically important commodity of the twentieth century because industrial civilization organized itself around its availability. Petroleum powered transportation, mechanized warfare, agriculture, manufacturing, and the growth of modern cities. The enormous wealth generated by its production transformed corporations and nations, while dependence on its supply created new forms of economic and military vulnerability.

The history of oil is a recurring struggle between competition and control. Discoveries attract investment and encourage production, but the resulting abundance frequently destroys prices. Producers respond by consolidating, restricting output, or seeking government intervention. These arrangements provide temporary stability until technological developments, new discoveries, or political changes disrupt the system again.

Power within the industry has repeatedly shifted toward whoever controlled its most important bottleneck. In the nineteenth century, the decisive advantages were refining efficiency, railroads, and distribution networks. During the first half of the twentieth century, access to concessions and control over international production became central. By the 1970s, producing governments had gained the ability to influence prices through ownership and production decisions. Throughout these changes, transportation capacity, spare production, and commercial relationships remained critical.

Oil's importance cannot be understood through economics alone. Governments have treated petroleum as a military necessity, an instrument of foreign policy, and a symbol of national sovereignty. Private companies consequently found themselves operating in a world where commercial decisions could alter diplomatic relationships and where political upheaval could destroy decades of investment.

The book's third story concerns the development of Hydrocarbon Man. The automobile transformed where people could live, petroleum products reshaped industrial production, and inexpensive energy supported increasingly elaborate patterns of consumption. Oil became so deeply embedded in ordinary life that people rarely noticed how much of their civilization depended on it.

# Part I: The Founders

## Chapter 1: Oil on the Brain — The Beginning

The modern petroleum industry began with the search for a better source of artificial lighting. By the middle of the nineteenth century, industrialization and population growth had created enormous demand for illumination. Whale oil was expensive, while cheaper substitutes were often smoky, unreliable, or dangerous. A fuel that could produce good light at a reasonable price had an enormous potential market.

George Bissell recognized an opportunity in rock oil, a substance that seeped naturally from the ground in western Pennsylvania. It was collected in small quantities and sold mainly as a medicinal remedy under names such as Seneca Oil. During a visit to Dartmouth College, Bissell encountered a sample and imagined that petroleum might be refined into an inexpensive illuminating fuel. The commercial opportunity arose not from discovering a new substance but from recognizing a more valuable use for something already known.

Bissell and his partners hired Yale chemist Benjamin Silliman Jr. to evaluate petroleum's properties. Silliman's 1855 report demonstrated that rock oil could be distilled into several valuable products, including a high-quality illuminating oil. The report helped attract investors by giving scientific credibility to an otherwise speculative venture. Yet proving that petroleum was useful did not solve the more difficult problem of obtaining it in commercial quantities.

The decisive idea was to adapt salt-well drilling to petroleum extraction. Instead of relying on oil that naturally seeped to the surface, investors could attempt to reach underground reservoirs directly. Edwin Drake was hired to carry out the experiment near Titusville, Pennsylvania. He possessed little relevant technical experience and was selected partly because he happened to know one of the investors. The company even gave him the fictitious title of colonel to make him appear more authoritative to the local population.

Drake spent months struggling with equipment, financing, and skeptical workers. Most people considered the project absurd, and the investors eventually instructed him to close it down. On August 27, 1859, just before the order reached him, his drilling team struck oil at approximately sixty-nine feet. The discovery established that petroleum could be produced by drilling rather than collected from natural seepages.

The industry's commercial foundation was already largely in place before Drake's discovery. Refiners knew how to produce illuminating oil, kerosene lamps were available, and consumers wanted cheaper lighting. Drake supplied the missing element: a method for obtaining sufficient raw material. The new industry emerged because an innovation in extraction connected technologies and markets that already existed.

Success quickly produced a different problem. Entrepreneurs rushed to Pennsylvania, bought leases, and drilled wells. Output expanded faster than the market could absorb it, causing petroleum prices to collapse. The industry had moved almost immediately from worrying about insufficient supply to suffering from excessive production.

American property law intensified this instability through the rule of capture. A landowner could extract oil from beneath his property even when the petroleum came from a reservoir shared with neighbors. Every producer therefore had an incentive to pump as rapidly as possible before someone else drained the pool. Individually rational behavior produced waste, premature declines in reservoir pressure, and destructive competition.

The rise and collapse of Pithole illustrated the speculative atmosphere. After major discoveries in 1865, the settlement grew from wilderness into a bustling town of approximately fifteen thousand people. Hotels, banks, shops, and land speculation appeared almost overnight. When production declined, people abandoned the town as rapidly as they had arrived. A parcel of land that sold for $2 million in 1865 was auctioned for $4.37 in 1878.

Transportation soon became another constraint. Teamsters initially moved oil barrels over muddy roads to railroad stations and could charge extraordinary prices because producers had few alternatives. Pipelines offered a cheaper and more reliable method of transport. Teamsters resisted the new infrastructure through threats and sabotage, but pipelines gradually displaced their business. The value of oil depended not just on its discovery but on the ability to move it economically.

Drake himself never captured a meaningful share of the wealth his discovery created. After unsuccessful business ventures, he lost his money and spent his later years in poverty, eventually receiving a small pension from Pennsylvania. Bissell became wealthy, while others built fortunes by refining, transporting, financing, and marketing petroleum. The uneven distribution of rewards foreshadowed a defining characteristic of the industry: those who organized the commercial system could earn far more than those who made the original discoveries.

## Chapter 2: Our Plan — John D. Rockefeller and the Combination of American Oil

John D. Rockefeller entered oil refining during a period when nearly anyone with modest capital could establish a refinery. Many operators were underfinanced, inexperienced, and obsessed with speculative profits. Rockefeller approached the business differently. He concentrated on efficiency, accounting, consistent quality, and the ability to survive periods of low prices.

In 1865, Rockefeller and his partner Maurice Clark disagreed over how aggressively their business should expand. They decided to auction the refinery between themselves, with the highest bidder taking ownership. Rockefeller won at $72,500, gaining complete control at the age of twenty-six. He later regarded the transaction as the beginning of his extraordinary business success.

Rockefeller's early advantage came from relentless attention to costs. He monitored expenditures, avoided unnecessary waste, maintained substantial cash reserves, and reinvested profits in larger and more efficient facilities. While many competitors operated from one sale to the next, his financial discipline allowed him to invest through downturns and acquire distressed assets.

He also recognized the advantages of controlling activities surrounding refining. Standard Oil acquired barrel-making operations, storage facilities, tank cars, shipping equipment, and distribution networks. These investments reduced dependence on outside suppliers and gave the company greater control over the cost and reliability of moving petroleum to market.

Henry Flagler played a critical role in developing Standard Oil's transportation strategy. His negotiations with railroads secured favorable freight rates, exploiting the company's volume and the railroads' desire for predictable business. Transportation became an advantage that smaller refiners found difficult to match.

The South Improvement Company scheme pushed this strategy much further. Participating refiners would receive rebates on their own freight shipments and additional payments calculated from the rates paid by nonparticipating competitors. Standard Oil could therefore benefit when rivals shipped oil through the same railroad system. The plan aligned Rockefeller's desire for industrial consolidation with the railroads' desire to end their own destructive rate wars.

When the arrangement became public in 1872, independent producers and refiners reacted furiously. Thousands gathered to denounce what they saw as an attempt to monopolize the industry. Although the scheme collapsed under public pressure, Rockefeller continued expanding through acquisitions and negotiations with competitors.

Rockefeller viewed consolidation as the solution to the industry's chronic instability. Too many refiners competing for the same market generated price wars that weakened everyone. By bringing production and distribution under coordinated management, he believed he could eliminate waste and establish a more rational industry.

His critics saw the other side of this strategy. Competitors could be pressured by unfavorable transportation rates, excluded from attractive commercial relationships, or forced to sell when their finances weakened. Standard Oil's efficiency was real, but so was its ability to use accumulated power to make competition increasingly difficult.

Rockefeller preferred centralized control because the economic advantages reinforced one another. Larger operations reduced unit costs, lower costs enabled aggressive competition, greater sales volume strengthened transportation bargaining power, and favorable transportation arrangements made further expansion easier. The company developed a self-reinforcing advantage that extended beyond any single refinery.

Standard Oil also established a worldwide distribution system for kerosene. It created recognizable products, consistent packaging, dependable quality, and commercial relationships in markets far from American oil fields. The company's international reach showed that organizing production and distribution could be more important than owning the original resource.

Rockefeller became one of the richest individuals in modern history, but his fortune generated enormous public hostility. His career embodied the achievements and contradictions of late-nineteenth-century capitalism: exceptional managerial discipline, technological progress, lower consumer costs, concentrated corporate power, and ruthless competition.

## Chapter 3: Competitive Commerce

The American oil industry rapidly became international because demand for kerosene existed far beyond the places where petroleum was produced. Supplying customers in Europe and Asia required shipping networks, warehouses, merchants, and distribution arrangements. The ability to transport oil economically became as important as the ability to extract and refine it.

Russia emerged as the first great challenge to American dominance. Around Baku, on the Caspian Sea, enormous petroleum deposits offered the possibility of production on a scale comparable to Pennsylvania. Yet Baku's location created major transportation problems. Oil had to cross difficult geography before it could reach international consumers.

The Nobel brothers helped transform Baku through investment in production, refining, and transportation. Ludwig Nobel introduced important operational improvements and developed specialized tanker transport on the Caspian. The family's success showed how engineering and logistics could turn geographically isolated reserves into commercially valuable resources.

The Rothschild banking family also became involved in Russian petroleum, providing capital and supporting infrastructure required to reach foreign markets. The entry of powerful financial interests demonstrated that modern oil production increasingly depended on institutions capable of making large investments long before receiving returns.

Marcus Samuel recognized an opportunity to challenge established distribution networks by transporting Asian petroleum efficiently. His business developed purpose-built tankers and helped establish Shell as an important international competitor. The successful movement of oil through the Suez Canal was especially consequential because it reduced the cost of reaching European markets from Asia.

The conflict among American, Russian, and Asian suppliers transformed petroleum into an integrated international business. Producers could no longer evaluate their prospects solely by the quantity of oil beneath their land. Their competitiveness depended on shipping rates, foreign customers, distribution infrastructure, and the strategic decisions of companies operating on other continents.

## Chapter 4: The New Century

At the beginning of the twentieth century, the petroleum industry's future was uncertain. Electric lighting threatened kerosene, which had been the industry's principal source of demand. The technology that made oil commercially valuable in the nineteenth century appeared increasingly vulnerable to replacement.

The internal combustion engine transformed petroleum's prospects. Gasoline, once a relatively unimportant refining product, became the fuel for automobiles. Its value increased dramatically because engineers developed machines capable of converting it into useful mechanical power.

The automobile created a market much larger and more durable than the original market for artificial lighting. As vehicles became affordable, petroleum companies expanded refining capacity, distribution networks, and retail operations. The industry increasingly served mobility rather than illumination.

The rise of Texas petroleum challenged the existing geography of production. Major discoveries demonstrated that enormous resources existed outside Pennsylvania and the established fields of the previous century. New discoveries repeatedly overturned assumptions about scarcity and opened opportunities for companies that had not participated in the industry's earliest development.

The discovery at Spindletop in 1901 was especially dramatic. A powerful gusher announced the arrival of a new producing region and stimulated the growth of enterprises that would become important competitors in American petroleum. The industry entered the new century with new fuels, new producers, and expanding markets.

## Chapter 5: The Dragon Slain

Standard Oil's dominance eventually provoked a political response. Reformers increasingly argued that the company had accumulated excessive economic power through acquisitions, secret transportation arrangements, and practices that undermined competition.

Investigative journalism helped make Standard Oil a symbol of the dangers of concentrated corporate power. Ida Tarbell's detailed examination of the company drew attention to the methods through which Rockefeller had built his empire.

The controversy reflected a broader transformation in American attitudes toward industrial capitalism. Large corporations had increased productive efficiency, but their influence over markets and political institutions raised questions about the appropriate limits of private power.

In 1911, the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil dissolved into separate companies under federal antitrust law. The decision marked a major assertion of government authority over the organization of an essential industry.

The breakup did not eliminate the economic advantages Rockefeller had developed. The successor companies inherited refining capacity, management expertise, commercial relationships, and valuable market positions. Many became substantial international enterprises in their own right.

The dissolution changed the structure of corporate ownership without ending the industry's tendency toward scale and integration. Petroleum continued to reward companies capable of coordinating expensive infrastructure, production, transportation, and distribution across large markets.

## Chapter 6: The Oil Wars — The Rise of Royal Dutch, the Fall of Imperial Russia

Royal Dutch and Shell developed into formidable competitors to Standard Oil by combining access to Asian petroleum with international shipping and distribution capabilities. Their eventual combination created a business with the geographic reach and financial resources necessary to compete in the global market.

Henri Deterding played a central role in building Royal Dutch/Shell. He approached petroleum as an international contest in which reserves, transportation, financing, and political relationships had to be coordinated under a unified strategy.

Russia demonstrated the hazards of operating in politically unstable producing regions. Baku's enormous oil fields attracted investment and technical expertise, but labor unrest, violence, and revolutionary movements repeatedly disrupted the industry.

The Russian Revolution fundamentally altered the position of foreign investors. Petroleum assets that companies had spent decades developing became subject to a radically different political system and claims of state ownership.

The episode showed that commercial property rights were not independent of political authority. Oil companies could possess valuable wells, refineries, and infrastructure yet lose effective control when the government that protected their ownership disappeared.

## Chapter 7: Beer and Skittles in Persia

William Knox D'Arcy obtained a broad petroleum concession in Persia in 1901. The opportunity was enormous, but almost everything about the venture was uncertain. The terrain was difficult, infrastructure was primitive, and commercial deposits had not been established.

Exploration became an expensive test of patience. George Reynolds directed drilling operations under challenging conditions while investors grew frustrated with the absence of discoveries. Financial pressures repeatedly threatened to end the project.

In 1908, oil was discovered at Masjid-i-Suleiman. The breakthrough established the commercial basis for the Anglo-Persian Oil Company and opened a major new producing region.

The discovery transformed the strategic importance of Persia. Britain's growing naval interest in petroleum meant that a commercial exploration venture was becoming connected to the defense of the British Empire.

The concession also established an enduring political problem. Foreign companies possessed extensive rights over a resource located within a sovereign country. As the value of petroleum became clearer, the disparity between foreign control and domestic ownership would become increasingly difficult to sustain.

## Chapter 8: The Fateful Plunge

Winston Churchill became First Lord of the Admiralty in 1911 as Britain faced an escalating naval rivalry with Germany. The Royal Navy's dependence on coal appeared increasingly disadvantageous because oil-powered vessels offered greater speed, efficiency, and operational flexibility.

The decision to convert the fleet was strategically dangerous. Britain possessed abundant domestic coal but depended on foreign sources for oil. The Royal Navy would gain technological superiority while losing part of its energy independence.

Churchill decided that the military benefits justified the risk. Britain could not preserve naval supremacy by refusing to adopt a superior fuel simply because its supply was less secure.

In 1914, the British government acquired a controlling interest in the Anglo-Persian Oil Company. The investment secured a stronger position in the supply of petroleum needed for naval operations.

The decision established a precedent for direct government participation in petroleum. Oil was becoming too important to national security for the British government to rely exclusively on ordinary commercial purchases.

# Part II: The Global Struggle

## Chapter 9: The Blood of Victory — World War I

World War I transformed petroleum from a rapidly growing commercial product into an essential military resource. Motor vehicles, aircraft, submarines, and oil-powered warships expanded the operational possibilities of modern armed forces.

The internal combustion engine increased military mobility while creating dependence on continuous fuel supplies. Armies could move equipment and soldiers more quickly, but mechanized operations became vulnerable to disruptions in petroleum transportation.

The Allied powers relied heavily on American oil production. The United States possessed the industry's most important productive base and supplied much of the petroleum required to sustain the war effort.

Tankers, pipelines, refineries, and distribution networks became strategic infrastructure. Oil beneath the ground had little military value unless it could be processed and delivered to the forces that needed it.

By the end of the war, governments understood that access to petroleum affected military capability and national independence. Energy security became a permanent concern of international strategy.

## Chapter 10: Opening the Door on the Middle East

The collapse of the Ottoman Empire created an opportunity for foreign governments and petroleum companies to reshape access to Middle Eastern resources. Mesopotamia attracted considerable interest because of the possibility that large reserves lay beneath its territory.

The Turkish Petroleum Company became the vehicle through which several competing international interests attempted to secure participation. The negotiations involved British, French, American, and other commercial claims, with each party seeking a favorable position before the scale of future discoveries was known.

Calouste Gulbenkian became indispensable because he could navigate these competing claims. His role was not primarily geological or operational. He possessed relationships, historical knowledge, and negotiating skill that allowed him to influence agreements among much larger organizations.

Gulbenkian eventually secured a five-percent interest, earning his famous reputation as Mr. Five Percent. His wealth reflected the value of arranging access to an opportunity rather than directly producing petroleum.

The discovery at Baba Gurgur near Kirkuk in October 1927 made the commercial stakes undeniable. The well erupted into a spectacular gusher, threatening nearby settlements and requiring more than a week to bring under control. With large petroleum reserves now confirmed, the partners had stronger reasons to resolve their disputes.

The 1928 agreement divided participation among the major groups, leaving Gulbenkian with his five percent. It also included a self-denying provision that restricted the participants from independently pursuing certain opportunities within the agreed territory.

Gulbenkian later described drawing a thick red line around much of the former Ottoman Empire to establish the geographical scope of the arrangement. Yergin suggests that the story may have embellished a boundary already under discussion, but the resulting Red Line Agreement became an important restriction on the participants' future activities.

An arrangement intended to reduce conflict over one producing region also constrained competition across a much wider area. The companies gained stability and shared access but surrendered some freedom to pursue attractive opportunities independently.

## Chapter 11: From Shortage to Surplus — The Age of Gasoline

The rapid spread of automobiles changed petroleum consumption. Gasoline demand increased as cars moved from luxury products toward mass ownership, creating new requirements for refining capacity, distribution, and retail infrastructure.

The expanding market produced recurring fears that petroleum supplies would prove inadequate. Estimates of reserves appeared limited relative to the potential growth of automobile ownership, and companies feared being unable to satisfy future demand.

These concerns encouraged exploration and technical innovation. New discoveries repeatedly increased available supply, demonstrating how estimates of future scarcity could change when investment and technology altered the amount of commercially recoverable petroleum.

Refining technology became increasingly important because crude oil contained different fractions that did not automatically match consumer demand. Techniques for producing more usable gasoline from available crude improved the industry's capacity to serve the automobile market.

Petroleum was also reshaping the economy beyond its direct sales. Roads, service stations, vehicle manufacturing, and suburban development created complementary investments that reinforced the growth of gasoline consumption.

## Chapter 12: The Fight for New Production

As international demand expanded, oil companies increasingly competed for concessions in promising regions. The most valuable opportunities often required substantial investment in countries whose political institutions and infrastructure were unfamiliar to foreign investors.

Venezuela became a major producing country after important discoveries attracted international capital and technical expertise. Its emergence diversified global petroleum supply and created new commercial relationships between producing countries and international companies.

Mexico demonstrated the political challenges surrounding foreign ownership of natural resources. Nationalist movements increasingly regarded petroleum as a source of national wealth that should not remain under extensive foreign control.

The development of the industry therefore involved negotiations over more than royalties. Foreign companies wanted long-term stability and protection for investments, while governments sought greater revenue, economic independence, and political authority over their resources.

## Chapter 13: The Flood

The discovery of the enormous East Texas oil field in 1930 created a surge of production during the Great Depression. Demand was already under pressure, and the additional supply drove petroleum prices sharply downward.

The crisis damaged producers across the industry. Companies with expensive operations or heavy debts could not survive prolonged low prices, yet individual producers remained reluctant to reduce output because they feared losing revenue and market share.

The government increasingly intervened to regulate production and prevent waste. American petroleum policy moved toward systems of allowable production designed to reconcile the volume extracted with the market's ability to absorb it.

Production regulation changed the economic meaning of capacity. An oil field could possess the physical ability to produce more petroleum than regulators permitted it to sell. Unused capacity would later become important for national security when international supplies were disrupted.

## Chapter 14: Friends — and Enemies

The major international oil companies recognized that unrestricted competition could undermine their profitability. They sought agreements that would stabilize markets, coordinate commercial activity, and reduce destructive rivalry.

The companies cooperated because they often needed access to one another's production, refineries, or distribution systems. Yet each also wanted to expand its own position and preserve independence wherever possible.

Their relationships therefore combined partnership and suspicion. Agreements could manage competition temporarily, but changes in production, demand, or political conditions repeatedly created incentives to renegotiate them.

The industry's preference for coordination reflected its unusually large fixed investments and volatile prices. Companies that committed substantial capital to long-lived infrastructure were especially vulnerable when market conditions changed unexpectedly.

## Chapter 15: The Arabian Concessions

Arabia initially attracted less petroleum investment than regions where discoveries had already established commercial potential. Exploration was expensive, infrastructure was scarce, and the existence of substantial reserves remained uncertain.

Frank Holmes helped stimulate interest in Arabian petroleum by connecting local rulers with foreign investors. His efforts showed how intermediaries could influence the development of resources before their value was fully understood.

Saudi Arabia eventually granted important concessions to American petroleum interests. The American companies invested in exploration and infrastructure despite uncertainty about whether the search would succeed.

Commercial discoveries in 1938 transformed the kingdom's prospects. Petroleum revenues would eventually provide the Saudi state with the resources to finance rapid development and exercise influence far beyond its borders.

The American presence in Saudi Arabia also established an unusually consequential relationship between private petroleum companies and a foreign monarchy. Commercial access, political stability, and strategic interests became closely connected.

# Part III: War and Strategy

## Chapter 16: Japan's Road to War

Japan developed a modern industrial economy and powerful armed forces without possessing sufficient domestic petroleum resources. Its military capabilities depended on imports, making foreign supply a major strategic vulnerability.

Japanese expansion in China and Southeast Asia created increasing conflict with Western powers. The United States and other countries used economic restrictions to oppose Japanese aggression, eventually threatening access to the oil required by Japan's military and industry.

The embargo confronted Japanese leaders with a strategic dilemma. Accepting restrictions would require changing their expansionist policies, while refusing to retreat could force them to seek petroleum supplies through military conquest.

The Dutch East Indies offered a potential solution because of its substantial reserves. Japanese planners sought to seize these resources, but they feared that American naval power could interfere with the operation.

The attack on Pearl Harbor was intended to weaken the American Pacific Fleet while Japan expanded southward. The attempt to overcome energy insecurity helped produce a war that ultimately made Japan's position far more precarious.

## Chapter 17: Germany's Formula for War

Germany faced its own petroleum problem. Its military doctrine emphasized mobility and mechanized operations, but domestic crude oil production could not adequately support a prolonged modern war.

German industry invested heavily in synthetic fuel production using coal. These processes reduced dependence on imported petroleum and supported the operation of aircraft, armored vehicles, and other military equipment.

The technology came at a substantial cost. Synthetic fuel facilities required capital, industrial equipment, and energy, and their concentrated infrastructure became vulnerable to Allied bombing.

The invasion of the Soviet Union was partly shaped by the objective of capturing the Caucasus oil fields. German planners regarded access to additional petroleum as essential to sustaining the country's military ambitions.

Germany's inability to secure sufficient supplies became increasingly damaging as the war continued. Fuel shortages eventually constrained training, transportation, aircraft operations, and armored warfare.

## Chapter 18: Japan's Achilles' Heel

Japan succeeded in capturing important petroleum-producing territories in Southeast Asia, but possession of the fields did not solve its strategic problem. The oil still had to be processed and transported to the places where it could be used.

Allied submarine warfare increasingly disrupted Japanese tanker shipping. Oil accumulated in producing regions while military and industrial consumers elsewhere faced shortages.

The destruction of transport capacity made Japan's petroleum strategy increasingly ineffective. A country could control valuable reserves and still be unable to sustain its armed forces.

As fuel became scarce, ships remained in port, aircraft training was curtailed, and industrial activity suffered. The limitation was no longer merely the amount of petroleum Japan possessed but the inability to deliver usable fuel consistently.

## Chapter 19: The Allies' War

American petroleum production provided the Allies with an enormous strategic advantage. The United States possessed large oil fields, advanced refineries, extensive transportation infrastructure, and the industrial capacity to expand output.

Supplying the war required close cooperation among petroleum companies, military planners, and government agencies. Production had to be coordinated with refining requirements, tanker availability, and the changing needs of military operations.

The Allies also targeted the enemy's energy infrastructure. Attacks on German synthetic fuel plants, refineries, and transportation systems contributed to the deterioration of Axis military capabilities.

The war demonstrated that advanced weapons were only as useful as the logistical systems supporting them. A tank without gasoline or an aircraft without aviation fuel represented industrial achievement without operational power.

# Part IV: The Hydrocarbon Age

## Chapter 20: The New Center of Gravity

After World War II, the Middle East emerged as the central region of future petroleum production. Enormous discoveries revealed reserves that could often be developed at exceptionally low cost.

The scale of these resources changed the economics of the international industry. Companies with access to Middle Eastern oil could produce large quantities at costs that many older fields could not match.

Saudi Arabia became especially significant because of the size of its reserves and the potential for sustained production. The kingdom's position would eventually give it influence over the global balance of supply and demand.

The geographical shift also created strategic vulnerability for consuming countries. Western Europe and Japan were becoming increasingly dependent on petroleum produced far beyond their own territories.

## Chapter 21: The Postwar Petroleum Order

The reconstruction of Europe and Japan created enormous demand for energy. Petroleum supported industrial growth, transportation, electricity generation, and rising living standards.

The major international oil companies built an integrated system connecting producing countries with distant consuming markets. Their operations combined production, refining, shipping, and distribution across multiple continents.

These companies often exercised extraordinary influence because they possessed the technical expertise and infrastructure needed to develop petroleum resources. Producing governments controlled the territory, but foreign enterprises frequently controlled much of the commercial system.

American foreign policy increasingly treated petroleum availability as essential to global economic stability. The success of friendly industrial economies and the political alignment of producing countries became connected to the reliability of oil supplies.

## Chapter 22: Fifty-Fifty — The New Deal in Oil

Producing countries increasingly questioned whether the existing concession system gave them a fair share of petroleum wealth. Foreign companies earned substantial profits, while host governments often received comparatively limited revenues.

Venezuela helped establish the principle that producing governments could claim approximately half the profits from petroleum operations. The formula became an attractive benchmark for other countries seeking greater revenue without necessarily taking over production.

Saudi Arabia pressed Aramco for a similar arrangement in 1950. The companies initially resisted because the existing concessions protected them from major changes in taxation.

The American foreign tax credit system provided an unexpected solution. Qualifying income taxes paid to Saudi Arabia could reduce the taxes owed by the American parent companies to the United States Treasury. Payments could effectively be redirected toward Riyadh without creating an equivalent increase in the companies' total tax burden.

Yergin illustrates the mechanism with a calculation based on 1949 figures. Aramco had paid approximately $39 million in Saudi royalties and owed approximately $43 million in American taxes. If an additional $39 million were paid as qualifying Saudi income taxes, the American tax liability could fall by roughly that amount. Saudi Arabia would receive substantially more money while the combined tax burden on the company changed much less.

American diplomats supported the shift because they feared that continued dissatisfaction could destabilize the Saudi monarchy and threaten American interests. The State Department was willing to accept reduced American tax receipts in exchange for a stronger political relationship and more secure access to petroleum.

The fifty-fifty agreement transformed the relationship between host governments and international companies. Producing countries had demonstrated that they could obtain a larger share of petroleum income while preserving foreign investment and technical participation.

## Chapter 23: Old Mossy and the Struggle for Iran

Iran became a major center of resistance to foreign control over petroleum. Many Iranians regarded the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's position as incompatible with national sovereignty and the fair distribution of the country's wealth.

Mohammed Mossadegh emerged as the leading political advocate of nationalization. In 1951, Iran moved to take control of the industry, challenging British commercial interests and the international petroleum order.

The confrontation became an economic crisis because ownership of oil fields alone did not guarantee the ability to sell their output. Iran faced difficulties replacing the technical, commercial, and distribution relationships through which its petroleum had reached international markets.

Britain opposed nationalization and sought to defend its interests, while the United States increasingly viewed the crisis through the lens of Cold War competition. Iranian politics became entangled with concerns about Soviet influence and regional stability.

Mossadegh was overthrown in 1953, and a new consortium arrangement subsequently restored substantial participation by international oil companies. Iran retained formal ownership, but foreign companies again played central roles in operating and marketing petroleum.

The crisis illustrated the difference between legal ownership and practical commercial control. Nationalization could transfer title to a resource without immediately transferring the capabilities needed to produce revenue from it.

The political consequences outlasted the commercial dispute. Foreign involvement in Iranian affairs contributed to grievances that would remain important long after the immediate petroleum arrangements had changed.

## Chapter 24: The Suez Crisis

Western Europe's dependence on Middle Eastern petroleum made the Suez Canal an essential transportation route. Tankers could reach European markets more efficiently through the canal than by sailing around Africa.

In 1956, Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the canal, challenging British and French interests. The resulting confrontation became a major international crisis and exposed the declining ability of European powers to impose their preferred political arrangements.

The disruption created serious logistical problems for petroleum supplies. Oil continued to exist in producing regions, but moving it to European consumers required longer routes and more tanker capacity.

Governments and oil companies organized emergency measures to redirect shipments and supplement supplies. American production and international commercial networks helped reduce the effects of the transportation interruption.

The crisis encouraged investment in larger tankers capable of moving petroleum economically around the Cape of Good Hope. An immediate disruption therefore accelerated technological and commercial changes that later made the transportation system more flexible.

Suez demonstrated that the geography of transportation could shape geopolitical power even when petroleum production itself remained available. Control over a narrow passage could affect countries thousands of miles away.

## Chapter 25: The Elephants

Oil companies increasingly concentrated on discovering exceptionally large fields, known within the industry as elephants. A giant field could provide profitable production for decades and justify enormous exploration expenditure.

The economics of exploration encouraged companies to accept repeated failures in pursuit of a few major successes. A single enormous discovery could compensate for many unsuccessful wells.

Geological research, seismic methods, and improved drilling technology increased the industry's ability to identify promising structures. Yet uncertainty remained fundamental because underground conditions could never be known completely before exploration.

The search for elephants made petroleum an unusually capital-intensive business. Long periods of investment could precede revenue, and financial endurance became as important as technical capability.

## Chapter 26: OPEC and the Surge Pot

The formation of OPEC in 1960 represented an attempt by producing governments to strengthen their position against the international oil companies. Its members wanted greater influence over prices, production, and the revenues generated by their resources.

The organization emerged partly because companies had previously possessed considerable discretion in setting posted prices used to calculate government revenues. A reduction in those prices could weaken producing countries' finances even when petroleum continued to flow.

Collective action offered governments an alternative to negotiating separately with powerful international enterprises. By coordinating their positions, producers could potentially increase their bargaining power.

OPEC faced difficulties because its members had different economic needs. Countries with large populations and urgent spending requirements often wanted immediate revenue, while those with enormous reserves could afford to consider longer-term price and production strategies.

Saudi Arabia gradually acquired a distinctive role because it possessed exceptionally large reserves and substantial production capacity. Its ability to increase or decrease output gave it influence that could not be measured by reserves alone.

## Chapter 27: Hydrocarbon Man

The postwar expansion of automobile ownership transformed the physical structure of modern societies. Highways, suburbs, shopping centers, and commuting patterns developed around affordable personal transportation.

Cheap petroleum encouraged people to live farther from their workplaces and rely on automobiles for ordinary activities. Residential and commercial infrastructure increasingly assumed that gasoline would remain widely available.

The transformation extended beyond transportation. Petroleum and natural gas supported fertilizer production, plastics, synthetic materials, manufacturing, and the distribution of food and consumer goods.

The modern economy consequently became dependent on hydrocarbons in ways that were difficult for individual consumers to observe. Oil was present not only in the fuel tank but throughout the systems that produced and delivered everyday necessities.

The environmental consequences of this dependence became increasingly important. Pollution, resource consumption, and climate concerns challenged assumptions that greater fossil-fuel use necessarily represented progress.

Hydrocarbon society had become difficult to change because its infrastructure and habits reinforced one another. A consumer could choose a different vehicle more easily than an entire society could reorganize the location of homes, workplaces, factories, and transportation networks.

# Part V: The Battle for World Mastery

## Chapter 28: The Hinge Years — Countries Versus Companies

The balance of power between producing countries and international oil companies changed rapidly during the late 1960s and early 1970s. Governments became more assertive as they acquired technical knowledge, stronger institutions, and greater confidence in the value of their reserves.

Libya became an important setting for this transformation after Muammar Qaddafi seized power in 1969. The new government demanded higher petroleum revenues and threatened to restrict production if companies refused.

The Libyans recognized that not all companies were equally able to resist. Major international enterprises possessed diversified production networks, but Occidental Petroleum depended heavily on Libyan oil. A loss of production threatened Occidental's financial position far more severely than it threatened some of its larger competitors.

Libya reduced Occidental's permitted output, putting pressure on Armand Hammer to negotiate. The government understood that the company had fewer alternatives and could therefore be targeted more effectively.

A logistical accident unexpectedly strengthened Libya's position. In May 1970, a tractor ruptured the Tapline pipeline in Syria, interrupting an important route for Saudi petroleum to reach the Mediterranean. Tanker rates increased dramatically as companies scrambled to replace the lost transportation capacity.

Libya's proximity to European markets suddenly became much more valuable. Petroleum supplies existed elsewhere, but transporting them had become more difficult and expensive. The country gained bargaining power because its customers had fewer convenient alternatives.

Hammer attempted to secure support and replacement supplies from other companies but could not obtain the protection he wanted. He eventually negotiated directly under intense pressure, repeatedly traveling between Tripoli and Paris.

Libya's success encouraged demands from other producing governments. The international companies could no longer assume that their collective size and financial resources would protect them from a government prepared to exploit the vulnerability of an individual participant.

The Tehran Agreement of 1971 marked another turning point. The companies accepted a higher minimum government share of revenues and increases in posted prices. The agreement was intended to provide stability for several years, but the balance of negotiating power had already shifted.

The struggle increasingly moved beyond taxation toward participation and ownership. Producing countries wanted not merely a larger share of profits but a direct role in controlling the resources and operations within their borders.

## Chapter 29: The Oil Weapon

Arab governments had discussed using petroleum as a political weapon long before the famous embargo of 1973. An important earlier attempt occurred during the Six-Day War of 1967, when producers restricted exports to countries regarded as sympathetic to Israel.

The 1967 embargo failed to achieve its intended leverage because the international petroleum system possessed substantial flexibility. American producers increased output, other countries supplied additional oil, and companies reorganized transportation routes.

The United States had large quantities of production capacity held idle under domestic restrictions. During the crisis, regulators permitted additional output, enabling American production to rise by almost a million barrels per day. Venezuela, Iran, and other producers also increased supplies.

Larger tankers further reduced the disruption caused by the closure of the Suez Canal. The petroleum system had adapted to earlier transportation crises, and those adaptations now made it more resilient.

By August 1967, Arab production was actually higher than before the war as producers sought to maintain revenues and market share. The embargo was abandoned after failing to produce the desired political effect.

The market was fundamentally different in 1973. World petroleum consumption had been growing rapidly, spare capacity had diminished, and consuming economies were more dependent on Middle Eastern supplies.

When war broke out between Israel and Arab states in October 1973, producing countries again considered using oil to influence Western policy. Output restrictions and embargoes became part of a broader effort to exert pressure on governments supporting Israel.

The United States' military assistance to Israel intensified the confrontation. Saudi Arabia and other Arab producers imposed restrictions that transformed a regional war into a global economic crisis.

The embargo included both general production reductions and selective restrictions against specific countries. The production cuts affected the worldwide supply balance, while the embargo attempted to influence the behavior of particular governments.

Oil prices rose dramatically, contributing to inflation, economic disruption, and widespread anxiety about energy security. The producing countries demonstrated that their control over marginal supply had become a powerful source of political influence.

The contrast between 1967 and 1973 was decisive. Similar political actions produced very different results because the underlying supply conditions had changed. The effectiveness of the oil weapon depended less on declarations than on the market's ability to replace withdrawn barrels.

## Chapter 30: Bidding for Our Life

The 1973 crisis generated fear that exceeded the immediate physical loss of petroleum. Governments, companies, and consumers could not confidently predict how long restrictions would last or whether additional producers would reduce output.

Buyers responded by competing for supplies and seeking protection against future shortages. This behavior intensified market pressure because demand for inventories increased at the very moment available production was constrained.

The disruption exposed how little reliable information participants possessed about the international oil balance. Uncertainty over inventories, production, transportation, and political decisions made planning difficult and encouraged exaggerated fears.

The international companies faced a complicated allocation problem. Their operations connected many producing regions with many consuming countries, but political restrictions now determined which customers could receive particular supplies.

Companies had to reroute petroleum while balancing commercial obligations and the demands of governments. Decisions that would normally have been logistical became politically sensitive because they affected the relative security of different countries.

An American government assessment later concluded that the companies' informal allocation efforts had been relatively equitable, while noting the extraordinary political responsibilities they had been forced to assume.

The crisis demonstrated that coordination networks can acquire political authority during emergencies. Whoever decides how scarce supplies are distributed can influence national outcomes even without formally exercising governmental power.

## Chapter 31: OPEC's Imperium

The oil shocks transformed the financial position of petroleum-exporting countries. Governments that had previously faced severe budget constraints suddenly received enormous revenues from their natural resources.

The new wealth financed infrastructure, industrial projects, public services, and ambitious economic development programs. Oil revenues allowed some governments to undertake transformations that would otherwise have been financially impossible.

The rapid influx of money also created difficulties. Governments expanded spending commitments, imported large quantities of goods, and sometimes developed projects whose economics depended on permanently high petroleum revenues.

The Shah of Iran pursued particularly ambitious modernization and military programs. Petroleum wealth increased the state's capacity to spend and intervene in society, but it also encouraged expectations and political tensions that the government struggled to manage.

OPEC's influence did not mean that its members possessed identical interests. Countries with urgent revenue requirements often preferred different pricing and production strategies from those possessing large reserves and greater financial flexibility.

The organization's power depended on cooperation among governments that remained politically independent and economically diverse. Collective influence could be substantial, but it was not the same as centralized control over the world's petroleum industry.

## Chapter 32: The Adjustment

The dramatic increase in petroleum prices encouraged consumers and businesses to reduce their dependence on oil. Investments in efficiency became more attractive because energy savings now had greater economic value.

Automobile manufacturers developed more fuel-efficient vehicles, industrial companies modified production processes, and governments introduced conservation policies. These adjustments occurred gradually because much of the relevant infrastructure had long useful lives.

High prices also encouraged exploration in regions that had previously appeared too expensive. North Sea production, Alaska, and other new sources reduced the relative importance of established Middle Eastern suppliers.

Alternative energy technologies and substitutions among fuels received greater attention. The energy system began adapting to the assumption that petroleum might remain expensive and politically vulnerable.

These responses gradually changed the balance between supply and demand. The economic incentives created by high prices were undermining the conditions that had originally allowed producers to charge them.

## Chapter 33: The Second Shock — The Great Panic

The Iranian Revolution of 1979 produced another major disruption in international petroleum markets. Political upheaval interrupted production and exports from one of the world's most important suppliers.

The physical loss of Iranian oil was significant, but uncertainty amplified its effects. Buyers feared that supplies might decline further or that political instability could spread to other producing countries.

Companies and governments competed for available petroleum, increasing inventories where possible. The resulting demand for security intensified price movements and created a widespread sense of crisis.

The revolution also challenged assumptions about the relationship between petroleum wealth and political stability. Iran had received extraordinary oil revenues, yet those resources had not prevented the collapse of the monarchy.

The second oil shock reinforced efforts by consuming countries to diversify supply, improve efficiency, and develop strategic petroleum reserves. Energy security became a lasting concern rather than a temporary response to the first crisis.

## Chapter 34: We're Going Down

By the early 1980s, the petroleum market was changing. New production had entered the market, while conservation and weaker economic conditions reduced the growth of demand.

OPEC attempted to support prices through production restrictions, but individual members faced strong incentives to maintain revenue. Reducing output could protect the market price while leaving the burden of adjustment on whichever producer was willing to sacrifice sales.

Saudi Arabia increasingly assumed that burden. Its production fell substantially as it tried to stabilize prices while other suppliers continued competing for customers.

The arrangement became difficult to sustain because Saudi Arabia was losing market share without receiving sufficient cooperation from other producers. Its ability to influence prices was constrained by competitors' willingness to expand output.

The problem resembled the industry's earliest experience in Pennsylvania. Every producer benefited from a stable market, but each also had an incentive to increase its own sales when others restrained production.

## Chapter 35: Just Another Commodity?

The organization of the international petroleum trade changed significantly as traditional long-term pricing arrangements weakened. Oil increasingly traded through spot markets in which buyers and sellers negotiated transactions based on current conditions.

Financial futures developed as mechanisms for managing exposure to changing prices. Producers, refiners, traders, and consumers could use contracts to hedge risks associated with future purchases or sales.

The growth of financial markets also introduced new participants whose primary interest was price movement rather than physical petroleum operations. Expectations about future supply and demand became increasingly visible through market prices.

Oil began to resemble other internationally traded commodities, with prices responding continuously to transactions and financial information rather than being determined primarily through administrative arrangements.

The change did not eliminate petroleum's geopolitical importance. Governments remained concerned about supply disruptions, military requirements, and the strategic influence of major producers even as commercial pricing became more market-oriented.

## Chapter 36: The Good Sweating — How Low Can It Go?

By the middle of the 1980s, Saudi Arabia had grown frustrated with its role as the producer sacrificing output to defend prices. Other suppliers continued selling petroleum while Saudi production declined.

The kingdom shifted toward defending its desired production volume and recovering market share. This required a strategy that could encourage customers to buy more Saudi oil even if the resulting competition weakened prices.

Netback pricing became an important instrument. Rather than charging refiners a fixed price for crude oil, Saudi Arabia linked its receipts to the market value of refined products while guaranteeing the refiner a specified margin.

Under such arrangements, a refiner could earn an agreed profit per barrel even when the market price of products fell. Selling greater volume therefore became attractive without the same incentive to defend a higher selling price.

The pricing structure encouraged throughput and intensified competition in an already oversupplied market. Saudi Arabia accepted greater exposure to falling prices in exchange for the possibility of recovering production volume.

Oil prices collapsed in 1986, reversing many of the economic relationships established during the 1970s. Producing countries faced sharply lower revenues, companies reduced investment, and consuming economies benefited from cheaper energy.

The episode demonstrated that market power depended on production decisions as well as reserves. A country attempting to defend a high price could eventually lose influence if competitors captured its customers and reduced its share of supply.

The collapse also revealed the limits of attempts to stabilize a market through collective restraint. Even governments with enormous reserves had to respond to the incentives created by competing suppliers, changing demand, and the commercial arrangements through which petroleum reached consumers.

# Epilogue: The Persistence of Oil

By the end of the 1980s, lower prices and changing international politics encouraged the belief that petroleum had become less strategically important. The industry appeared increasingly governed by competitive markets rather than by the political power of producing countries.

Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990 challenged that assumption. Saddam Hussein's attempt to seize a neighboring oil-rich country threatened to concentrate extraordinary petroleum resources under the control of a single aggressive government.

The international response demonstrated that petroleum remained central to questions of national security and geopolitical power. The possibility of one state dominating the Persian Gulf was unacceptable to countries whose economic and military systems depended on access to its resources.

At the same time, environmental concerns were beginning to transform the debate over petroleum. Fossil fuels had supported enormous improvements in material prosperity, but pollution and climate change raised questions about the long-term consequences of hydrocarbon dependence.

The world therefore faced a problem different from the one that had launched the industry. Nineteenth-century entrepreneurs had struggled to find enough oil to serve emerging markets. By the end of the twentieth century, societies had become so dependent on petroleum that reducing its use required changes across much of modern economic life.

# Synthesis

The history of oil is not simply a succession of discoveries, wars, and price changes. It is a history of shifting constraints. The early industry struggled to obtain enough petroleum, then to transport and refine it efficiently. Large corporations emerged by controlling the infrastructure and commercial relationships surrounding production. Governments later challenged corporate authority as the political and economic value of their natural resources increased.

Some of the industry's most consequential arrangements arose from problems outside petroleum production itself. Railroad rate structures helped Rockefeller consolidate American refining. Tax provisions enabled Saudi Arabia to obtain a larger share of Aramco's revenues. A pipeline accident strengthened Libya's negotiating position. Tanker capacity and unused American production helped defeat the 1967 embargo. Contractual incentives contributed to the price collapse of 1986.

These episodes reveal how the value of a resource depends on the institutions through which it is produced and exchanged. Ownership matters, but so do contracts, regulation, transportation, financial structures, technology, and the availability of alternatives.

Oil also repeatedly confounded predictions about scarcity and abundance. Fears of shortage encouraged exploration, investment, and technological development. New supplies then weakened prices and undermined the economics of earlier investments. High prices encouraged conservation and substitution, while low prices stimulated consumption and discouraged new production.

Petroleum wealth proved equally capable of strengthening and destabilizing governments. Large revenues could finance economic development and military power, but they could also encourage excessive spending, distort incentives, and create political expectations that governments could not sustainably fulfill.

The strategic importance of oil depended on the difficulty of replacing it when supply was interrupted. The failed embargo of 1967 and successful price shock of 1973 showed that the effectiveness of energy coercion depended on spare capacity, inventories, transportation flexibility, and the immediate alternatives available to consumers.

The deepest transformation occurred outside the petroleum industry itself. Cheap oil enabled societies to construct transportation systems, cities, industrial processes, and everyday routines that assumed continued access to concentrated liquid energy. The more successful this civilization became, the more difficult it was to change its foundations.

The enduring lesson of *The Prize* is that a resource becomes powerful through the economic and political systems built around it. Petroleum's importance came not simply from what lay underground, but from the technologies that made it useful, the institutions that controlled it, the nations that depended on it, and the civilization that grew around its consumption.
