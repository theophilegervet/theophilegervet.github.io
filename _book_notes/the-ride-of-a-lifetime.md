# Bob Iger — The Ride of a Lifetime

## Operating Playbook

**1. Find the few things that matter, then organize the company around them.** As CEO, Iger set three priorities: branded content, technology, and global expansion. He used them to guide capital allocation for over a decade. Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, Shanghai Disneyland, BAMTech, and Fox were expressions of the same strategy. Strategic consistency compounded.

**2. Recognize value before it is obvious, then bet big.** Disney's animation was deteriorating; Iger bought Pixar rather than attempt another incremental turnaround. Marvel's characters were underexploited; Disney could turn them into global franchises. He looked for exceptional assets whose value Disney was uniquely positioned to unlock.

**3. Build relationships before transactions.** Iger repaired Disney's relationship with Steve Jobs before proposing Pixar. Jobs later helped establish credibility with Marvel. George Lucas required years of personal attention. Iger understood that founders weren't selling financial assets alone; they were entrusting someone with their life's work.

**4. Buy excellence without destroying it.** Pixar's value wasn't merely its films but its people and creative process. Iger gave its leaders authority over Disney Animation rather than absorbing Pixar into the existing bureaucracy. The acquisition was a mechanism for importing exceptional judgment.

**5. Be secure enough to admit ignorance, decisive enough to lead anyway.** Appointed head of ABC Entertainment without Hollywood experience, Iger asked basic questions, relied on expert colleagues, and learned quickly. He didn't confuse authority with expertise, or humility with indecision.

**6. Stay calm while demanding exceptional work.** Roone Arledge taught Iger to reject mediocrity and exhaust alternatives. He also demonstrated how fear and erratic criticism damage organizations. Iger preserved the standards while trying to eliminate the volatility.

## Part I — Learning

### ABC Sports — Roone Arledge

Arledge transformed sports coverage by recognizing that television was storytelling, not simply showing competitions. He relentlessly improved production quality, experimented with technology, and demanded solutions when something seemed impossible.

Iger's first major operating education: **care obsessively about the product, not the process that produced it.**

But Arledge often withheld feedback until the last minute and intimidated employees. Iger saw how brilliance could coexist with unnecessary organizational dysfunction.

### Capital Cities — Murphy and Burke

Capital Cities acquired ABC and stripped out executive excess. Tom Murphy and Dan Burke were intensely cost-conscious but willing to spend on exceptional talent.

They decentralized decisions, gave people responsibility beyond their experience, and promoted Iger repeatedly because they trusted his intelligence and temperament.

**Their insight: bet on people's capacity rather than their résumé, then give them genuine authority.**

### ABC Entertainment — Learning Creative Judgment

Iger inherited a struggling network without knowing how to evaluate scripts. He immersed himself in the work, asked questions, and depended on experienced executives.

He championed *Twin Peaks*, recognizing something distinctive despite its commercial uncertainty.

The lesson was not to trust every instinct. It was to **develop enough taste to recognize originality, and enough conviction to support it when consensus is against you.**

### Disney Succession — Reorienting the Company

Under Michael Eisner, Disney became increasingly centralized and politically dysfunctional. Relationships with Pixar and other creative partners deteriorated.

Iger initially struggled to persuade the board he should succeed Eisner. His candidacy improved when he shifted from defending past performance to explaining Disney's future.

He proposed three priorities: high-quality branded content, embracing technology, and international growth.

**The CEO's job is to make a small number of consequential choices about where the company goes next.**

## Part II — Leading

### Pixar — The Defining Acquisition

Disney's animation capability had weakened. Pixar had what Disney needed: world-class creative talent, technology, and a repeatable process for producing great films.

Iger repaired the relationship with Steve Jobs, negotiated the $7.4B acquisition, and gave Pixar's leadership responsibility for revitalizing Disney Animation.

The key wasn't merely buying Pixar's existing IP. **Disney acquired the capability to consistently create new IP.**

### Marvel — The Power of Dormant Assets

Marvel had thousands of characters, but much of their potential remained unrealized.

Iger saw that Disney's distribution, marketing, consumer products, and global infrastructure could multiply their value. The $4B acquisition was a bet on decades of future stories rather than current earnings.

**Look for assets whose potential is constrained by the capabilities of their current owner.**

### Lucasfilm — The Limits of Trust

Iger spent years cultivating George Lucas before acquiring Lucasfilm for roughly $4B.

Lucas cared deeply about Star Wars and its future. Disney ultimately chose not to follow his proposed direction for the sequels, creating disappointment despite the relationship Iger had built.

**A founder's emotional attachment can be central to getting a deal done, but post-acquisition control must be understood clearly.**

### Streaming — Cannibalizing the Existing Business

Disney's television networks generated enormous profits, but Iger recognized that direct-to-consumer distribution threatened the entire model.

He invested in BAMTech, committed Disney to streaming, and pursued Fox to acquire additional content and international scale.

The difficult decision was not recognizing streaming's potential. It was **investing aggressively in a future that threatened Disney's existing cash flows.**

### Shanghai — Operating Through Crises

Shanghai Disneyland required roughly eighteen years of negotiations, political navigation, and capital investment. Iger maintained the vision of a park that was authentically Disney but adapted to China.

Its opening coincided with severe tragedies in Orlando. Iger had to compartmentalize, delegate operational responses, and intervene personally where leadership responsibility demanded it.

**Sustained execution requires both extraordinary persistence and the ability to distinguish what needs your attention from what needs your team's expertise.**

## Synthesis

Iger combined three capabilities that rarely coexist:

- **Strategic judgment:** Identifying which assets, capabilities, and market shifts would matter over decades.
- **Human judgment:** Winning the trust of founders and recognizing extraordinary creative talent without needing to be the creative genius himself.
- **Operating discipline:** Maintaining simple priorities, demanding high standards, and executing patiently through uncertainty.

**His defining skill was recognizing what Disney needed to become, acquiring the capabilities to get there, and preserving the people and cultures that made those capabilities valuable.**
